# Highly composite numbers

*Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society*, second series 14,
347–409. The [primary text](https://ramanujan.sirinudi.org/Volumes/published/ram15.html)
uses $d(N)$ for the positive divisor count, denoted $\tau(N)$ here.

Section 32, equations (182)–(183), defines a superior highly composite
integer using $d(N)/N^\epsilon$, with $\epsilon>0$ and the stated convention
for comparisons at tied prices. Sections 33–34, equations (185)–(197),
give the prime exponent thresholds and the equivalent stack-of-primes
description. A strictly interior price gives a unique maximizer.

[Table 1](https://ramanujan.sirinudi.org/Volumes/published/ram15_tab1.png)
lists $5040=2^4 3^2 5\,7$ with $d(5040)=60$ and marks it as superior
highly composite. It also supplies the corresponding divisor record.
The application at $\epsilon=3/10$ uses exact adjacent integer thresholds;
it is an application of this classical theory, not a new extremal theorem.

This objective differs from the colossally abundant objective
$\sigma(n)/n^{1+\epsilon}$. Neither the divisor-count price nor the
record supplies a uniform Robin inequality or a physical cost law.
