# OEIS A359482

The entry defines the lexicographically earliest sequence of distinct positive
terms whose adjacent sum is a substring of their decimal concatenation. It asks
whether this is a permutation. Hasler's July 3, 2023 comment conjectures that
it is not, and that the initial 1 is its only single-digit term. The same
comment makes a stronger assertion about multiples of ten below 100; the
first-part module does not address that assertion. The full published proof and
its formal extension are described below.

## Proof scope

Mathlib supplies digit length monotonicity, base evaluation, concatenation,
and list infix lemmas. The proof of the target theorem is a repository
derivation by Ma and Zhang. Hasler's independent published proof covers both
parts. No publication priority or external coauthorship is claimed here.

## Mathematical scope

The repository proof handles all positive x and all 1<=d<=9. Reversing the
decimal lists leaves either the entire old operand or a shifted list. In the
shifted case, evaluation forces a*10^length(r)=9*value(r); reduction modulo
nine gives a=0 or a=9, and each contradicts positivity or the remainder bound.
This includes the empty remainder list, so one-digit x is covered.

Greedy totality is proved separately: repeating the decimal block of x and
then appending a block of zeros yields arbitrarily large legal successors.
Thus a positive unused successor always exists. The sequence is defined by
the original minimum rule, and the missing positive integer 2 proves failure
of surjectivity.

### Mathematical correspondence

For x>0 and 1<=m<10, the formal classification is Legal(x,10m) iff x=m.
The reverse decimal concatenation begins with the digits 0,m followed by
the digits of x. Put q=10^length(digits(x+10m)). A substring beginning at
the first of these positions gives 99x=qR. Coprimality of q and 99 forces
q to divide x, contradicting 0<x<q. Beginning one digit later gives
9(x-m)=qR; coprimality with 9 forces x=m. An occurrence entirely in the
digits of x forces x+10m=x. Conversely x=m is legal for each of the nine
positive one-digit values of m.

This formulation retains the exceptional predecessor x=m in the middle
position. The sequence conclusion then uses its actual preceding term:
the initial term is 1 and every term from index 2 is at least 10. Hence
20,30,...,90 are absent. The least-unused rule chooses 10 at index 2.
Consequently a positive index with value 10m exists exactly when m=1.
