# The first root-limit question in OEIS A195417

## Verified locator

The source is [OEIS A195417](https://oeis.org/A195417/internal),
by Paul D. Hanna and OEIS contributors, author line Sep 17 2011.
The [OEIS JSON entry](https://oeis.org/search?q=id:A195417&fmt=json)
contains the definition and the first question:

> a(n) = sum of the absolute values of the coefficients in (1+x-x^3)^n.
>
> What is the limit a(n)^(1/n)? The limit appears to be near 2.657...

The adjacent question concerns successive ratios and their attractors.
It is outside the root-limit statement considered here. The integer
polynomial and the coefficientwise natural absolute values are preserved:
the sum runs from index zero through 3n, before casting to the reals.
At n=0 the sum is one. The limit uses real powers and the entire
natural-number sequence.

## Mathematical answer and prior theory

Write s=sqrt(13), C=(97+26s)/27 and M=sqrt(C). For |z|=1 and u=Re(z),

$$
|1+z-z^3|^2=f(u)=5+8u-4u^2-8u^3,
\qquad
C-f(u)=\frac{(6u+1-s)^2(6u+1+2s)}{27}.
$$

Since u>=-1 and s>=5/2, the latter expression is nonnegative.
Equality is attained at u=(s-1)/6 and z=u+i sqrt(1-u^2).
This u lies in [-1,1], so z is a unit complex number.
Thus M is the exact unit-circle maximum modulus.

The original CountableHankelNegativeWitness.eval_bound gives M^n<=a(n).
The generic A192205RootLimit.power_sum_sq_le combines finite Fourier
Parseval with Cauchy-Schwarz; at most 3n+1 support indices yield

$$
a(n)^2\le(3n+1)M^{2n},\qquad
M^n\le a(n)\le(3n+1)M^n.
$$

Taking real nth roots for n>=1 and using the original
A192205RootLimit.degree_root_limit proves

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}a(n)^{1/n}
=\sqrt{\frac{97+26\sqrt{13}}{27}}.
$$
