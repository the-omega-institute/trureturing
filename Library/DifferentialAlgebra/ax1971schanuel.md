# Differential transcendence of the logarithmic exponential series

## Verified locator

James Ax, *On Schanuel's conjectures*, Annals of Mathematics 93 (1971),
252–268, DOI [10.2307/1970774](https://doi.org/10.2307/1970774).
The publisher record at https://annals.math.princeton.edu/1971/93-2/p03
identifies the article, pagination and differential-algebraic scope.
The exponential formulation is also stated in the paragraph following
Theorem 1.1 of Jonathan Pila and Jacob Tsimerman, *Ax-Schanuel for the
j-function*, arXiv:1412.8255v2, p. 1. That paragraph distinguishes Ax's
exponential theorem from their modular theorem. No numbered theorem in
Ax's paper is attributed to the special series below.

## Application of Ax's theorem

In the differential field of complex meromorphic germs at zero, put
$E=\exp(t)$ and $v=\log(2-E)$, with $v(0)=0$. The constant field is
$\mathbb C$. The differential form of Ax's theorem says that exponential
pairs $y_i'=y_i x_i'$ with $y_i\ne0$ and $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ linearly independent over
$\mathbb Q$ modulo the constants satisfy

$$
\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb C}
\mathbb C(x_1,\ldots,x_n,y_1,\ldots,y_n)\ge n+1
$$

for one derivation and nonconstant $x_i$.
Use $(x_1,y_1)=(t,E)$ and $(x_2,y_2)=(v,2-E)$.
If $at+bv$ is constant for $a,b\in\mathbb Q$, differentiation gives
$2a-(a+b)E=0$. Since $E$ is nonconstant, $a=b=0$.
Ax's bound is therefore three. Since $y_1+y_2=2$, the field is
$\mathbb C(t,E,v)$, so $t,E,v$ are algebraically independent.
The case $n=1$, with $(x_1,y_1)=(t,E)$, already gives the classical
transcendence of $E$ over $\mathbb C(t)$.

Put $F=-v$. Then $F'=E/(2-E)$ and
$E=2F'/(1+F')$. Thus the fields generated by $(t,E,v)$ and $(t,F,F')$
agree. Algebraic independence over $\mathbb C$ implies that over
$\mathbb Q$. Taylor expansion preserves these identities; a polynomial
relation of the formal Taylor series would be a relation of germs.
The Lean theorem concerns the rational formal series, not an analytic
convergence theorem or the entire operadic counterexample.

## Classical differential-algebra ingredients

The direct formal proof uses standard differential-algebra arguments
instead of formalizing Ax's theorem. For a reduced rational function
$p(u)/q(u)$ with $u'=u$, the putative primitive equation for $u/(2-u)$ is

$$
(2-u)(p'q-pq')=u q^2.
$$

Evaluation at $u=2$ forces $q(2)=0$ and $p(2)\ne0$. Writing
$q=(u-2)^m s$ with $m>0$ and $s(2)\ne0$, cancellation and another
evaluation at two give $2m p(2)s(2)=0$, a contradiction.
This local pole argument works for any characteristic-zero coefficient
field, including one with a nonzero derivation.

Algebraic primitive descent is likewise classical: differentiate the monic
minimal polynomial $Y^n+a_{n-1}Y^{n-1}+\cdots$ of an algebraic primitive
with derivative $g$ in the base field. The differentiated relation has
degree below $n$, so minimality makes it zero. Its next-to-leading
coefficient is $a_{n-1}'+ng=0$, and $-a_{n-1}/n$ is a base-field primitive.
This is the elementary descent step underlying integration in finite
terms; see Maxwell Rosenlicht, *Integration in Finite Terms*, American
Mathematical Monthly 79 (1972), 963–972,
DOI [10.1080/00029890.1972.11993166](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029890.1972.11993166).
The title, author and pagination of this contextual reference are confirmed
by its bibliographic record; no exact theorem number or verbatim statement
is assigned to it here.

Extension of a derivation to fractions, its quotient rule and compatibility
with further embeddings are standard localization facts. The Lean
construction uses Mathlib's localization universal property and dual
numbers; agreement and commutation are consumed bind-only helpers.
The rational change $e\mapsto e/(2-e)$ preserves transcendence by its
inverse $g\mapsto 2g/(1+g)$, and adjoining a transcendental element to a
transcendence tower is a standard algebraic-independence fact.
