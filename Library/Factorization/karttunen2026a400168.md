# The A400168 existence question

The sequence has offset one and NAME:

> a(n) = A235224(A003415(n)) - A400164(n), where A235224 is the length of primorial base representation, A400164(n) = Max_{d|n, d is a prime power} A235224(n/d), and A003415 is the arithmetic derivative.

Its COMMENT asks:

> Question: Are there terms greater than 1?

This is an existence question. The universal inequality used as the formal
claim is its negative answer, not a conjecture asserted by Karttunen.

Write $P_0=1$ and let $P_k$ multiply the first $k$ primes. A002110 explicitly
uses this indexing. A235224's NAME sets $L(0)=0$ and, for positive $m$, takes
the largest $k$ with $P_{k-1}\le m$. Equivalently, $L(m)$ is the least natural
$k$ with $m<P_k$: strict increase gives
$L(m)=j+1$ exactly when $P_j\le m<P_{j+1}$ for $m>0$.
The least threshold exists without a finite cap, since
$P_{m+1}\ge\operatorname{nthPrime}(m)\ge m+2$. The Lean definition uses
Mathlib's value-bounded `primorial` at the zero-based `Nat.nth Nat.Prime k`,
not at the prime count $k$ itself.

A003415 sets $D(0)=D(1)=0$ and has the factorization formula
$D(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}v_p(n)(n/p)$ for positive $n$, equivalent to its prime
values and product rule. The Lean definition uses the finite support of
`n.factorization`; all its quotients are exact and the empty factorizations
supply the zero and one cases.

A400164's FORMULA maximizes $L(n/d)$ over every divisor in A246655, whose NAME
specifies $p^e$ with $p$ prime and $e\ge1$. Its COMMENT explicitly contrasts
these with exponent-zero powers, excluding one. The finset supremum gives
$M(1)=0$ from the empty filter and equals this maximum for all $n\ge2$.
The extension $M(0)=0$ is outside the offset-one domain.

Thus the original question is exactly
$\exists n\ge1,\ L(D(n))>M(n)+1$. Negating
$\forall n\ge1,\ L(D(n))\le M(n)+1$ has this same meaning. For natural
lengths, either integer or truncated natural subtraction exceeds one
precisely under the displayed strict inequality.

The exact input $n=2^{49}=562949953421312$ has
$D(n)=13792273858822144$, $L(D(n))=15$ and $M(n)=13$, so its term is two.
This answers the question positively without asserting that it is the
first such input or that there are infinitely many.
