# OEIS A225004

The entry records, for each `n`, the largest multiple of `n` whose divisor count is smaller than
twice that of `n`. Its terms begin `1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 18, 49, 64, 81, 50, 121, 48, 169, …`.

## Reading of the statement

The entry's own worked example fixes the reading: "a(6) = 18 because 6 has 4 divisors and term must
have fewer than 8 divisors. Only 6, 12, and 18 are multiples of 6 with fewer than 8 divisors."

So a multiple `m` of `n` is admissible when `τ(m) < 2 τ(n)`, and `a(n)` is the largest admissible
multiple. The conjecture asks whether `a(n)` always divides `n^2`.

## Scope of the recorded answer

**Every** admissible multiple divides `n^2`, not only the largest, and one line of exponent
bookkeeping is the whole reason.

Write `f` for the exponent in the prime factorisation, so `τ(k) = ∏ (f_k(p) + 1)` over the primes of
`k`. Let `n ∣ m` with both positive, so `f_n ≤ f_m` pointwise and the primes of `n` are among those
of `m`. Suppose some prime `q` had `f_m(q) > 2 f_n(q)`.

- If `q` is not a prime of `n`, then `q` is a prime of `m` outside the primes of `n`. Splitting the
  product for `τ(m)` at that boundary, the part over the primes of `n` is at least `τ(n)` and the
  remaining part is at least `2`, the factor at `q` alone being at least two.
- If `q` is a prime of `n`, then `f_m(q) + 1 ≥ 2 f_n(q) + 2 = 2 (f_n(q) + 1)`. Pulling `q` out of the
  product over the primes of `n`, that single factor supplies the two and every other factor is at
  least the corresponding factor of `τ(n)`.

Either way `τ(m) ≥ 2 τ(n)`, contradicting admissibility. So `f_m(p) ≤ 2 f_n(p)` for every prime,
which is exactly `m ∣ n^2`.

The same argument shows the admissible multiples are finitely many, all of them being divisors of
`n^2`, so the largest one the entry names exists. The prime powers are the extreme case: for
`n = p^a` admissibility reads `b + 1 < 2(a+1)`, so `b ≤ 2a` and `a(p^a) = p^{2a} = n^2`.
