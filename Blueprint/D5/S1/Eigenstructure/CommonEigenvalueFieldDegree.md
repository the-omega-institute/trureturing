# Common Eigenvalue Field Degree

## Abstract

A common nonzero eigenvector over an extension field bounds the degree of the field generated by an arbitrary family of eigenvalues.

Let F and L be fields with an F-algebra structure on L, let J be finite and I arbitrary, and let M_i be J-by-J matrices over F. Suppose a single nonzero vector x in L^J satisfies M_i^L x=eigenvalue_i*x for every i. Scalar extension here means entrywise algebraMap F L; the matrices need not commute, be semisimple or admit a common eigenbasis. The extension L/F need not be finite or algebraic.

**Theorem 1.1 (The entire eigenvalue field has degree at most the matrix size).**

*Commentary.*

The actual intermediate field adjoin F (range eigenvalues) is finite-dimensional over F and has finrank at most card J. The family I may be infinite or empty. If J is empty, the nonzero-vector hypothesis is impossible. Zero and repeated eigenvalues are allowed; the proof never divides by an eigenvalue.

Normalize one nonzero coordinate of x to 1. The whole F-algebra A generated by the matrices preserves its L-line, by algebra-adjoin induction. Reading that coordinate gives an F-algebra character chi:A->L. Let W be the image of A under the selected-row linear map. Row evaluation restricts to a surjection W->chi(A), so the character image is finite-dimensional of dimension at most dim W<=card J, rather than the weaker full-matrix bound card J squared.

The image is a finite-dimensional commutative domain inside the actual field L. Mathlib's finite-domain-to-field theorem converts it to an intermediate field with the same carrier. Every supplied eigenvalue lies in that field, and intermediate-field inclusion transfers finite-dimensionality and the degree bound to the entire eigenvalue-generated field. No finite-generator hypothesis is imposed on I.

This theorem bounds the eigenvalue field, not the field generated by the normalized coordinates. For example, rational scalar matrices with vector (1,cube-root(2)) have rational eigenvalues even though their two coordinates generate a cubic field. The actual Hecke coefficient-field consumer proves a separate equality between its coordinate field and its eigenvalue field before applying this theorem.
