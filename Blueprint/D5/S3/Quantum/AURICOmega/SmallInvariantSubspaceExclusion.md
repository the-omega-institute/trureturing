# Exclusion of small invariant subspaces

## Abstract

In dimension at most four, a noncommutative group of linear isometries without nonzero common fixed vectors, generated by elements that send no nonzero vector to its negative, has only the trivial invariant subspaces.

**Theorem 1.1 (Invariant subspaces are trivial below dimension five).**

*Commentary.*

Let E be a finite-dimensional real inner product space with finrank E <= 4, and let U be a subgroup of the group of linear isometry equivalences of E. Assume three things. First, the only vector v with T v = v for every T in U is v = 0. Second, U equals the subgroup generated by the set of those T in U such that, for every v in E, T v = -v implies v = 0. Third, U contains elements S and T with S * T different from T * S. Then every real subspace X of E with T x in X for all T in U and all x in X is either the zero subspace or all of E. The proof uses three facts. The orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is invariant, because each element of U preserves the inner product and U is closed under inverses. On an invariant line each element of U acts by 1 or -1, since it preserves norms; a generator cannot act by -1, so the whole group fixes the line pointwise, and the first assumption excludes such a line. On an invariant plane, identified isometrically with the complex numbers, each element acts as a rotation z -> a z or a reflection z -> a conj z with |a| = 1. A reflection of the plane sends some nonzero vector to its negative, so every generator, and hence every element of U, acts on the plane by multiplication by a nonzero complex number; these actions commute. If X were neither zero nor all of E, then X and its orthogonal complement would both be invariant, of positive dimension and of total dimension at most four, and neither could be a line. Both would therefore be planes, every two elements of U would commute on each of them, and so on all of E, contradicting the third assumption.
