# Equal Gram Unitary Extension

## Abstract

Equal complex column Gram matrices admit a unitary taking one rectangular matrix to the other.

**Theorem 1.1 (One unitary on the whole common codomain).**

$$\forall A,B: Matrix\left(m, n, Complex\right),\ gram\left(B\right) = gram\left(A\right) \implies\ \exists U: Unitary\left(m, Complex\right), B = U A$$

*Proof.* Machine-checked in Lean as `D5/S3/Quantum/Algebra/GramUnitaryExtension.exists_unitary_mul_eq_of_conjTranspose_mul_eq` (`✓ std3`). ∎

*Citation.* Sirui Lu and TNLean contributors (2026). *Gram equality and unitary extension for rectangular matrices*. URL: <https://github.com/LionSR/QICLean/blob/cdaa636d1f41560f7caca7077c11068229cb9727/QICLean/Algebra/MatrixGramUnitary.lean>.

*Commentary.*

Let m and n be finite types, and A and B complex m-by-n matrices. If their column Gram matrices A* A and B* B agree, with star denoting conjugate transpose, there is a unitary U on the full m-dimensional codomain such that B=UA. Gram equality identifies all linear dependencies among the columns. It defines an isometry between their ranges through the quotient by the kernel, and finite-dimensional isometry extension supplies U. Rank-deficient matrices and empty column types are included.

For continuous matrix fields, this theorem applies separately at each point. Its conclusion does not supply a continuous choice of the unitary. The distinction already has a maximal norm obstruction on the unit sphere S in R cubed. Fix any finite n at least one, write zeta(s,t,r)=s+i t, and set Z=zeta I and W=abs(zeta) I. Both fields have norm one and their column Gram fields agree. Every continuous unitary field u on S nevertheless has an equatorial point p with norm(u(p)Z(p)-W(p))=2.

To see this, parameterize the northern hemisphere by beta(z)=(Re z, Im z, sqrt(1-normSq(z))) on the closed complex unit disk. This map is continuous, including at its boundary. The determinant of u composed with beta gives a disk filling of the determinant loop along the equator; radial contraction gives that loop winding number zero. With a(t)=exp(2 pi i t) and c(t)=(Re a(t), Im a(t),0), the loop V(t)=a(t)u(c(t)) has determinant a(t) to the n-th power times det(u(c(t))). Multiplicativity of winding number therefore gives winding(det V)=n, which is nonzero.

If every V(t) avoided minus one in its spectrum, its principal argument would be a continuous selfadjoint matrix field. The homotopy exp(i(1-s)arg(V(t))) would contract V through unitaries, making its determinant winding number zero. Hence minus one occurs in the spectrum at some t. The unitary spectral norm criterion and the triangle bound then give norm(V(t)-I)=2, which is precisely the equatorial error above. This argument needs neither a continuous choice of eigenvectors or eigenvalues, nor differentiability, nor a restriction on the parity of n.

The spectral criterion and continuity of the principal-argument functional calculus are supplied by Mathlib.Analysis.CStarAlgebra.Unitary.Connected. The integer degree and its free homotopy and product laws are available in TauCetiProject/TauCeti, revision 1e668f5e55bc20f2eb87f4ba49b48833470290d1, TauCeti/Topology/Circle/Degree.lean. These standard results carry the argument; the application introduces no new winding invariant.

The actual Alexandroff duplicate is also a specialization of D5.S3.Topology.Resolution.RetractiveAttachment. Set its core B and index type I both equal to S, each component P(p) to Unit, and the attachment t(p)(*) to p. Its carrier S plus Sigma p Unit identifies with S times {0,1}: core points are bottom points and component points are top points. Its basic set at a bottom point p consists of V on the bottom and V minus a finite set J on the top. This is exactly the duplicate topology. In one direction take J={p}; in the other replace V by V minus (J minus {p}). The latter is open because S is T1, still contains p, and its duplicate neighborhood omitting top p refines the given set.

The supplier's compact_space and hausdorff apply to these actual singleton attachments. Its continuous_retraction is the projection pi(p,e)=p, and core_closed_embedding is the bottom inclusion iota(p)=(p,0), with pi iota=id. component_clopen_embedding gives each top singleton clopen; is_closed_core makes the bottom closed and the top open. The sphere is connected and has distinct antipodal points. Mathlib.Topology.Perfect therefore makes every sphere neighborhood infinite. Removing finitely many points leaves a top point in each bottom neighborhood, proving that the top is dense. The bottom is a connected sphere copy and is not open; its distinct points also prevent the whole duplicate from being zero dimensional.

The points with a local basis of clopen neighborhoods are exactly the top. A top singleton is itself clopen. If a clopen set contains a bottom point p, its trace on the connected bottom sphere is nonempty and clopen, hence is the entire bottom. Choose a different sphere point q. The inverse image under pi of the complement of q is an open neighborhood of bottom p that excludes bottom q. No clopen neighborhood of bottom p can lie in it. This proves L(X)=T, so its closure is the whole duplicate, without giving a clopen basis at a bottom point.

On this compact duplicate, define the same Z=(zeta composed with pi) I and W=(norm(zeta) composed with pi) I as continuous matrix fields. Their norm is the supremum over the entire duplicate of the matrix operator norm. For every finite n at least one, norm(zeta)<=1 and the point (1,0,0) give norm(Z)=norm(W)=1; conjugate transpose and scalar multiplication give equal column Gram fields. The equal-Gram theorem supplies a unitary at every point, including both copies of each pole. Writing p+=(0,0,1) and p-=(0,0,-1), their common zero set {x : Z(x)=0 and W(x)=0} is exactly {(p+,0),(p+,1),(p-,0),(p-,1)}: for n at least one, Z(x)=0 if and only if zeta(pi(x))=0, which holds exactly over these two sphere poles. Restrict these pointwise choices to the top: its induced topology is discrete, so they form a continuous exact solution there. Density also implies that two continuous matrix fields agreeing on the top agree everywhere; this uniqueness statement does not assert existence of an extension.

Every global continuous U on the duplicate restricts to u=U composed with iota. At bottom p its error is literally norm(u(p)(zeta(p) I)-norm(zeta(p)) I), and evaluation bounds this by the whole-space supremum norm. Unitary multiplication preserves the operator norm, so norm(UZ-W)<=norm(Z)+norm(W)=2 on the entire duplicate. Thus a bottom equatorial error of two from the sphere argument gives whole-space error exactly two. The topological construction supplies the restriction and norm comparison; the equatorial spectral hit comes from the determinant and winding argument above.

For two global unitary fields U and V, multiply their difference UZ-VW by the adjoint of V. Norm invariance reduces it to (adjoint(V) U)Z-W, whose norm is two by the same universal obstruction. The function (A,B) maps to norm(A-B) is continuous in the whole-space norm. An equality of two on both actual orbits therefore extends first to the closure of one orbit and then to the closure of the other. This conclusion requires the universal obstruction for every global unitary; pointwise Gram equality and the dense-top solution alone do not give it. The local phase conj(zeta)/abs(zeta) is used only where zeta is nonzero; the two poles and their four duplicate points are never divided by their modulus.

The modulus identity is the positive square root identity in the same continuous matrix algebra. At x, functional calculus gives abs(Z(x))=norm(zeta(pi(x))) I=W(x), and W(x) is positive. Thus W is positive as a field, and W squared equals star(Z) Z by the scalar Gram computation. CFC.sqrt_eq_iff identifies W with the positive square root of star(Z) Z, hence W=abs(Z) in the whole field algebra. The zero values at both copies of both poles satisfy this identity as well.

On the open set where zeta composed with pi is nonzero, the phase conj(zeta)/norm(zeta) gives a continuous unitary scalar matrix taking Z to W. On the dense discrete top, pointwise equal-Gram choices give a continuous exact unitary solution, including its two pole points. If any such top solution had a continuous global unitary extension, the continuous error would vanish on the dense top and therefore everywhere. This contradicts the global error two. For an arbitrary global U, the continuous error norm is bounded by two and takes the value two at a bottom equatorial point. Its top-layer values have supremum two by density: every neighborhood on which the error exceeds two minus a positive epsilon meets the top. Both unitary orbits and their closures are nonempty, as witnessed by the identity field; every pair from the opposite closures has distance two by the relative-unitary and closure arguments above. In particular, the distance from W to the orbit of Z is two, and W lies outside its closure.
