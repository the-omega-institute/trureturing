# Positive semidefinite class functions of finite rank

## Abstract

On an arbitrary group, a class function whose kernel is positive semidefinite and whose translates span a finite-dimensional space is a combination with positive weights of the characters of finitely many pairwise inequivalent irreducible unitary representations, and the dimension of that span is the sum of the squared degrees.

*Commentary.*

Let G be an arbitrary group and phi a complex-valued function on G. Three hypotheses are made. First, the kernel whose entry at (x, y) is phi(x * y^-1) is positive semidefinite: it is Hermitian and its quadratic form is a nonnegative real number on every finitely supported vector; in the formula, of turns the function of x and y into a matrix indexed by G x G. Second, phi is a class function: phi(g * x * g^-1) = phi(x) for all g and x. Third, the sections of the kernel, the functions x maps to phi(x * y^-1) for y in G, span a finite-dimensional complex subspace of the functions on G.

The conclusion provides a natural number m, degrees n(i) for i in Fin(m), representations rho(i) of G on the Euclidean spaces EuclideanSpace(C, Fin(n(i))) and real weights c(i), with seven properties: every rho(i) is irreducible; rho(i) and rho(j) admit no equivalence of representations when i and j are distinct; every operator rho(i)(g) preserves the norm; every c(i) is positive; phi(x) is the sum over i of c(i), regarded as a complex number, times the character of rho(i) at x; every matrix coefficient g maps to the inner product of w and rho(i)(g)(v) lies in the span of the sections; and the dimension of the span of the sections is the sum of the squares n(i)^2. In the displayed formula the arguments in square brackets are the instance hypotheses of the statement.

No topology is assumed on G. If G is a topological group and phi is continuous, the representations rho(i) are continuous: each section x maps to phi(x * y^-1) is continuous, so every function in their finite-dimensional span is continuous, and by the matrix-coefficient property every matrix coefficient of rho(i) is such a function.

The proof begins with the reproducing kernel Hilbert space H of the kernel. Its kernel functions k(y), with k(y)(x) = phi(x * y^-1), span a dense subspace; evaluation identifies H injectively with a space of functions on G and carries this span onto the span of the sections, which is finite-dimensional, so the span is closed and equals H. Right translation, which sends a function h to x maps to h(x * g), maps k(y) to k(y * g^-1) and preserves the inner products of kernel functions because the kernel is invariant under right translation; it is therefore a representation pi of G on H by isometries, and the reproducing property gives phi(x) as the inner product of k(1) and pi(x)(k(1)).

A finite-dimensional representation by isometries is semisimple `D5/S3/FiniteGroups/GroupAlgebra/UnitaryCharacterKernel.isSemisimpleRepresentation_of_norm_map`. By strong induction on the dimension, splitting off a proper subrepresentation and a complement, every coefficient x maps to f(pi(x)(u)) with f linear is a finite sum of trace coefficients x maps to the trace of rho(j)(x) composed with B(j), for irreducible representations rho(j) by isometries on Euclidean spaces; an irreducible summand is carried to a Euclidean space by an orthonormal basis, and its coefficient f(pi(x)(u)) is the trace of pi(x) composed with the rank-one map w maps to f(w) u. Equivalent representations are then grouped, conjugating the operators B(j) into one representative of each class, so that the family becomes pairwise inequivalent.

Because phi is a class function, the difference of the trace sums at g * x * g^-1 and at x vanishes, so the families (rho(j)(g^-1) B(j) rho(j)(g) - B(j))_j pair to zero with every family (rho(j)(x))_j under the trace pairing with unit weights. By Burnside's theorem for the family these span the product of the endomorphism algebras `D5/S3/FiniteGroups/GroupAlgebra/CharacterKernelRank.span_range_pi_eq_top`, and the trace pairing is nondegenerate `D5/S3/FiniteGroups/GroupAlgebra/CharacterKernelRank.eq_zero_of_tracePairing`, so every B(j) commutes with rho(j). By Schur's lemma over the complex numbers B(j) is a scalar c(j), and phi is the sum of the c(j) times the characters. Since the kernel of phi is positive semidefinite, every c(j) is nonnegative `D5/S3/FiniteGroups/GroupAlgebra/UnitaryCharacterKernel.posSemidef_sum_character_kernel_iff`, and the representations with c(j) = 0 are dropped.

Writing the character of rho(i) at x * y^-1 as the trace of rho(i)(x) composed with rho(i)(y^-1), the sections are the images of the families (rho(i)(y^-1))_i under the linear map that sends a family A to the function x maps to the sum over i of c(i) times the trace of rho(i)(x) composed with A(i). By Burnside's theorem for the family, the span of the sections is the image of the whole product of the endomorphism algebras; choosing the family that is a multiple of the rank-one map z maps to the inner product of w and z times v in position i and zero elsewhere shows that every matrix coefficient lies in that span. The dimension formula is the rank of a weighted character kernel `D5/S3/FiniteGroups/GroupAlgebra/CharacterKernelRank.finrank_span_sum_character_kernel`.

No hypothesis can be dropped. On the integers, phi(n) = n is a class function whose sections are spanned by the constant function and the identity, but its kernel x - y is not Hermitian, and phi is unbounded while every combination of characters of finite-dimensional unitary representations is bounded. On the group SU(2) regarded as an abstract group, a diagonal matrix coefficient of the defining representation has a positive semidefinite kernel whose sections span a space of dimension two, but it is not a class function, and two is not a sum of squared degrees of pairwise inequivalent irreducible representations, because SU(2) is perfect and its only one-dimensional representation is trivial. On the integers, the indicator function of 0 is a positive semidefinite class function, its kernel being the identity matrix, but its sections are the indicator functions of single points and span an infinite-dimensional space. These examples are not part of the formal statement.

The result is the finite-rank case of the classical theory of functions of positive type on groups: a positive definite function whose associated cyclic representation is finite-dimensional, and which is invariant under conjugation, is a positive combination of irreducible characters. The statement here is for an arbitrary group, without topology, and includes the rank formula.
