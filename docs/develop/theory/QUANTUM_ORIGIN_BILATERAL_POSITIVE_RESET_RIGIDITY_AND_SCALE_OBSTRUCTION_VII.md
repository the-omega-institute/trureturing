## 98. Whole zero-effects and the actual tangent

**Lemma 98.1 (Local Hausdorff regularity).** Compact pure transitivity
on the whole base in (97.1), even without L3, implies that

$$
\mathcal B(p)=\{e\in E:e(p)=0\}
\tag{98.1}
$$

is locally Lipschitz in Hausdorff distance: at every $p$ there are
a relative neighborhood $U\subset P$ and $L<\infty$ such that
$d_{\rm H}(\mathcal B(q),\mathcal B(r))\le L|q-r|$ for all
$q,r\in U$. Any fixed finite-dimensional norms can be used.

Proof. The compact matrix group is Lie, and its orbit is smoothly
embedded as $H/H_p$. A local smooth section $\sigma:U\to H$
satisfies $\sigma(q)p=q$. On functionals let
$\varrho(g)e=e\circ g^{-1}$. Preservation of $C$ and $u$ gives
$\varrho(g)E=E$ and, in both directions,
$\mathcal B(gp)=\varrho(g)\mathcal B(p)$.
After shrinking $U$, smoothness in a local orbit chart and comparison
of chart and ambient distances give
$\|\varrho(\sigma(q))-\varrho(\sigma(r))\|\le L_0|q-r|$.
The interval $E$ is compact: its values on a basis selected from
the spanning compact $K$ are bounded in $[0,1]$, and it is closed.
Thus $M_0=\max_{e\in E}\|e\|<\infty$. Match the images of the
same $e\in\mathcal B(p)$ in both transported sets. Each directed
Hausdorff distance is at most $L_0M_0|q-r|$. This concerns the entire
zero-effect set, without differentiating a selected effect. ∎

**Lemma 98.2 (Span and tangent recovery).** Under the same weaker
compact pure-orbit hypotheses,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{span}\mathcal B(p)
 &=\{\ell\in V^*: \ell(p)=0,\ \ell|_{T_pP}=0\},\\
T_pP&=\ker u\cap\bigcap_{e\in\mathcal B(p)}\ker e.
\end{aligned}
\tag{98.2}
$$

Proof. Every $e\in\mathcal B(p)$ is nonnegative on $P$ and has

\tag{98.3}
$$

satisfies $h_0(q)=|q-p|^2/2$ on $P$. It is nonnegative on $K$
and $C$, and has no other pure zero. Taylor's theorem in a smooth

Double annihilation gives the annihilator
$\mathbb Rp+T_pP$. Its intersection with $\ker u$ is exactly

**Proposition 98.3 (Shared tangents and the exposure condition).**
Under (97.1)–(97.2), if $p\in\partial F\cap\partial G$ and

\tag{98.4}

## 99. Maximum-face quartics and sufficient isotropy

**Definition 99.1 (The invariant quartic).** Fix one invariant positive
definite $Q$ on all $W$, use its Euclidean second fundamental form
$\mathrm{II}^Q$, and put $a_Q=D_Q/2>0$. At $p\in P$ set

$$
\Psi_p(v)=|\mathrm{II}^Q_p(v,v)|_Q^2-a_Q^{-2}Q(v,v)^2,
\qquad v\in T_pP.
\tag{99.1}
$$

Let $\mathfrak h_p$ be the Lie algebra of the actual $H_p$ tangent
representation. For a maximum-radius actual face through $p$, set
$E_F=T_p\partial F$, a two-dimensional plane.

**Theorem 99.2 (Two alternative suppliers).** In the domain of
Definition 97.1, either of the following conditions at one anchor
$p$ forces every actual face radius in $Q$ to equal $a_Q$:

1. The $H_p$-invariant real homogeneous quartics on $T_pP$ form
   exactly $\mathbb R\,Q(v,v)^2$.
2. Some maximum face $F$ through $p$ and some $0\ne v\in E_F$
   satisfy $E_F+\{Av:A\in\mathfrak h_p\}=T_pP$.

Consequently the original-unit representation (97.3), with its entire
effect interval and all mixed-state evaluations, follows from either
supplier by VI90.1–90.2.

Proof. By [VI91.2][VI], every anchor lies on a maximum face of radius
$a_Q$, each such sphere is totally geodesic in $P$, and (99.1)
vanishes on its tangent plane. Equivariance of the Euclidean second
fundamental form makes $\Psi_p$ an invariant homogeneous quartic.
Under condition 1 it is $cQ(v,v)^2$, and a nonzero vector of a
maximum plane gives $c=0$.

Under condition 2 consider $H_p\times E_F\to T_pP$,
$(g,w)\mapsto gw$. At $(1,v)$ its derivative is
$(A,z)\mapsto Av+z$, which is onto. Its image contains a nonempty
open set. Invariance and vanishing on $E_F$ make $\Psi_p$ zero
on that open set, and the real polynomial identity principle gives
$\Psi_p=0$. This uses an open image, not all-tangent coverage by
face planes. Transitivity transports the identity to every anchor.
In particular every unit-speed complete geodesic $\gamma$ of $P$
has $|\gamma''|_Q=1/a_Q$. Compactness and connectedness give
completeness and geodesics joining arbitrary endpoints.

Here is a direct equality argument for the global radius step,
using the Euclidean chord comparison underlying
[Mendes, Lemma 1][Mendes], without its classification. Fix any
$s_0\in\mathbb R$, put $L=\pi a_Q$, and let $T(s)=\gamma'(s)$.
For $s,t\in[s_0,s_0+L]$, the spherical distance of the unit vectors
$T(s),T(t)$ is at most $|s-t|/a_Q\le\pi$. Hence

$$
Q(T(s),T(t))\ge\cos((s-t)/a_Q).
\tag{99.2}
$$

Integrating on the entire square gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\gamma(s_0+L)-\gamma(s_0)|_Q^2
&\ge\int_{s_0}^{s_0+L}\!\int_{s_0}^{s_0+L}
\cos((s-t)/a_Q)\,ds\,dt\\
&=2a_Q^2(1-\cos\pi)=4a_Q^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{99.3}
$$

The diameter bound gives the reverse inequality. The continuous
nonnegative difference in (99.2) therefore vanishes at every pair
$(s,t)$. Set $e_1=T(s_0)$ and $e_2=T(s_0+L/2)$; their Gram
matrix is the identity. Pairing $T(s)$ with them and taking a squared
norm gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(s)&=\cos((s-s_0)/a_Q)e_1+\sin((s-s_0)/a_Q)e_2,\\
\gamma(s)&=\gamma(s_0)+a_Q\sin((s-s_0)/a_Q)e_1
 +a_Q(1-\cos((s-s_0)/a_Q))e_2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{99.4}
$$

Thus every such interval traces a radius-$a_Q$ semicircle. Intervals
with positive-length overlap share a nonconstant circular arc and
three noncollinear points, fixing the same plane and center. Chains
of these overlaps identify one circle along the whole real parameter
line. Their derivatives agree on overlaps, so the parametrization
continues with the same orientation and period $2\pi a_Q$.
The complete geodesic has that entire circle as its image; intervals
sharing only an endpoint would not justify this step.

For any distinct $x,y\in P$, take such a circle $\Gamma$ through
them and its closed disk $B=\operatorname{conv}\Gamma\subset K$.
If $c$ is its center, then

$$
\left|\frac{x+y}{2}-c\right|_Q^2
=a_Q^2-\frac{|x-y|_Q^2}{4}<a_Q^2.
\tag{99.5}
$$

The actual face $F(x,y)$ contains this relative interior point of
$B$. For any $r\in B$, extend the segment from $r$ through that
interior point a little further inside $B$. The face property forces
$r\in F(x,y)$. Hence the entire disk and circle lie in the face,
whose diameter is at least $2a_Q$. Every pair radius is at most

constructed in $P$ before this face argument; it was not assumed

Now apply VI90.1: its global proper effects on the whole cone, zero
at every chart antipode, satisfy exactly the common-support interface
of [V88.10][V88] and [WholePairBallConfirmation][Confirm]. The full

## 101. Whole-sphere compression and low-rank obstructions

**Lemma 101.1 (Invariant projection compression).** For every nonzero
$H$-invariant subspace $U\subset W$, let $\Pi$ be its orthogonal
projection. It commutes with $H$, and $|\Pi(p-b)|=r_U>0$ on all
$P$. On each actual face,

$$
\Pi z_F\perp\Pi L_F,\qquad
\Pi_{L_F}\Pi\Pi_{L_F}=\kappa_F\Pi_{L_F},\qquad
0\le\kappa_F\le1.
\tag{101.1}
$$

Thus $\Pi|_{L_F}$ is zero or a scaled isometric embedding of the
whole three-dimensional direction space.

Proof. Orthogonality and invariance give commutation and the constant
projection norm. If that norm were zero, $U$ would be orthogonal
to the centered spanning pure orbit. On the entire sphere
$v\in L_F$, $|v|=\rho_F$, the expression
$|\Pi z_F+\Pi v|^2=r_U^2$ is constant. Subtraction at $v,-v$
gives the first identity. Its quadratic term is constant on the
whole sphere, so polarization gives scalar compression. Positivity
and contractivity of $\Pi$ give the stated scalar interval.
Zero compression is allowed separately for each face. ∎

**Theorem 101.2 (Dimensions one through three).** There is no nonzero
invariant subspace of dimension one or two. A three-dimensional
invariant subspace forces $U=W$ and the entire original $K$ to be
one actual three-ball face.

Proof. In dimensions one or two, nonzero compression would embed
the whole $L_F$ injectively in $U$, which is impossible. Therefore
all compressions vanish. Every pure secant belongs to its actual
face direction space, so $\Pi$ annihilates all pure secants. They
span $W$, contradicting $U\ne0$.

Now let $\dim U=3$. Some face has nonzero compression by the same
spanning argument. For every such face $\Pi L_F=U$, whence
$\Pi z_F=0$ and $\kappa_F\rho_F^2=r_U^2$. Its projected whole
ball is exactly $\overline B_U(r_U)$ and its projected boundary
is the entire $S_U(r_U)$. Consequently

$$
\Pi(P-b)=S_U(r_U),\qquad \Pi(K-b)=\overline B_U(r_U).
\tag{101.2}
$$

The map $\varphi:P\to S_U(r_U)$, $x\mapsto\Pi(x-b)$, is open,
including when $H$ is disconnected. For a reference $q$ with image
$a$, its stabilizers satisfy $H_q\subset H_a$. Compact orbit
maps identify the source and target with $H/H_q$ and $H/H_a$.
For an open subset of $H/H_q$, the inverse image of its image in
$H/H_a$ is a union of open right translates by $H_a$. The quotient
map, and therefore $\varphi$, is open.

Suppose distinct $p,q$ have the same projection $a$. Openness and
the absence of isolated points in the sphere give $q_j\to q$ with
$\varphi(q_j)\ne a$. Thus $F_j=F(p,q_j)$ has nonzero compression
and full projected ball. Its radii satisfy
$r_U\le\rho_j\le R$. Compactness of $K$ and of $\operatorname{Gr}_3(W)$
allows a subsequence with $c_j\to c$, $L_j\to L$, and
$\rho_j\to\rho\ge r_U$. The closed balls converge in Hausdorff
distance to the nondegenerate ball
$B=c+\rho\overline B(L)\subset K$. Its image is still the full
ball in (101.2), by continuity of linear projection. No assertion
that this limiting ball is an actual face is needed.

The points $p,q$ lie on its sphere by passage to the limit. Their
midpoint lies in $\operatorname{ri}B$. The original linear effect

$$
e_a(x)=\frac{u(x)}2+
\frac{Q_0(a,\Pi(x-u(x)b))}{2r_U^2}
\tag{101.3}
$$

belongs to $[0,u]$, since its normalized values on $K$ lie in
$[0,1]$ by (101.2). It is one at $p,q$ and their midpoint.
Yet on $B$ it ranges from zero to one, since $B$ projects onto the
full ball. An affine function bounded above by one on $B$ and equal
to one at a relative interior point must be constant there: vary
each affine direction in both signs. This contradiction proves
global injectivity of $\varphi$ on $P$.

One nonconstant face already has its entire boundary mapping onto
the sphere in (101.2). Every original pure point has that same
projection as some boundary point, so injectivity identifies all
$P$ with this boundary. Taking convex hulls gives $K=F$. Its full
affine span is $b+W$, and hence $\dim W=3$ and $U=W$. ∎

**Corollary 101.3 (Original rank, nullity and complementarity).**
Outside the whole-three-ball branch, every nonzero invariant
subspace and every nonzero eigenspace of an invariant self-adjoint
operator has dimension at least four. Every nonzero PSD
$D\in\mathcal M$ has rank at least four; if it is singular, its
nullity is also at least four. A nonzero singular obstruction
therefore needs $n\ge8$. If $A\succeq0$ annihilates $\mathcal M$
and $D\in\mathcal M$ is PSD, then $AD=DA=0$.

Proof. Invariant ranges, kernels and eigenspaces obey Theorem 101.2.
In the whole-three-ball case every pair face is $K$, and
$\mathcal M=0$, so there is no nonzero obstruction there either.
The nullity assertion concerns singular $D$ only; it puts no
positive lower bound on the nullity of a full-rank witness.
For complementarity,

$$
0=\operatorname{tr}(AD)
=\|A^{1/2}D^{1/2}\|_{\rm HS}^2.
\tag{101.4}
$$

Thus that product and its adjoint vanish, giving $AD=DA=0$ without
assuming commutativity. A singular PSD $A$ can induce a PD form
on a quotient by its kernel, but that changes the carrier and does
not satisfy (100.4) on all original $W$. ∎

## 102. Four-dimensional invariant summands

**Theorem 102.1 (The complementary-dimension bound).** In the domain
of Definition 97.1, every four-dimensional $J=H^0$-invariant
subspace $U\subset W$ forces $n\ge13$. In particular this holds
for every four-dimensional $H$-invariant subspace. Throughout this
statement the obstruction space remains the original $H$ space
$\mathcal M$ of (100.2).

Proof. VI89.3 makes $J$ pure-transitive on the same $P$, so
Theorem 101.2 applies with $J$ and the same $Q_0$. It follows that
$U$ is $J$-irreducible: a nontrivial decomposition would contain a
subspace of dimension at most three, and the whole-three-ball
alternative cannot contain $U$. We give the actual face and
representation arguments, including every complement of dimension
at most eight.

First, two geometric tests will be used. A face containing a relative
interior point of a convex subset of $K$ contains that whole subset,
by the segment-extension argument in the proof of Theorem 99.2.
If an actual group element acts as $-I$ on $W$, the midpoint of
$p$ and its image is the interior point $b$. Their actual face is
$K$, so L3 would give $n=3$.

Next suppose an actual $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ subgroup acts on
$W$ with only trivial modules and quaternionic fundamental modules.
Write a pure displacement as $f+(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$, where $f$ is
fixed and the $a_i\in\mathbb H$ transform by left multiplication.
If the tuple is nonzero, the real-linear map
$t\mapsto(ta_1,\ldots,ta_k)$ is injective on $\mathbb H$.
Invariance gives constant norm for unit $t$, and scaling gives norm
$|t|$ times that constant for all $t$. Its orbit is therefore a
whole round $S^3$ in an affine four-space, with its four-ball hull
inside $K$. Antipodal orbit points are original pures and their
actual three-ball face contains the relative interior center of
this four-ball. It would contain the four-ball, a contradiction.
The tuple is nonzero for some pure point whenever the action on
$W$ is nontrivial, by centered spanning. Thus such a nontrivial
fundamental-plus-trivial actual action is impossible.

Consider the image algebra $\mathfrak k\subset\mathfrak{so}(U)
\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)_L\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)_R$. It contains
an axis ideal $\mathfrak{su}(2)$. Here the compact-algebra facts
can be checked directly. The invariant positive product
$-\operatorname{tr}(XY)$ splits an ideal and its orthogonal
complement into commuting ideals. An abelian ideal is central:
its brackets with the algebra lie in it and, by invariance, are
orthogonal to it. Repeated splitting gives a center and simple
ideals. A commuting skew family on $\mathbb R^4$ preserves a real
two-plane and cannot act irreducibly. In the cross-product model
of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, a two-plane is not a subalgebra, so a
nonabelian subalgebra is all $\mathfrak{su}(2)$. A simple ideal
of $\mathfrak k$ therefore projects isomorphically onto one axis,
or is a graph of an automorphism between both. Such automorphisms
are orientation-preserving isometries of the cross-product algebra
and are inner. A graph is conjugate to the diagonal; its quaternion
action fixes the real line through $1$, and its centralizer in
$\mathfrak{so}(4)$ is zero. It cannot yield an irreducible image,
even after adding commuting ideals. An axis ideal follows.

This ideal acts in the actual $J$, not merely a quotient. For the
kernel of $\operatorname{Lie}J\to\mathfrak k$, an invariant
positive product supplies a commuting complementary ideal mapping
isomorphically onto $\mathfrak k$. Lift the axis there. Its
finite-dimensional compact $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ representation
integrates to the simply connected $\operatorname{SU}(2)$; its
image lies in $J$, since every element of $\operatorname{SU}(2)$
is an exponential and the image exponential is that of the lifted
ideal in the closed matrix group $J$. On $U$ this is the faithful
quaternionic fundamental action. Its central element $-1$ has image
$\tau$, a genuine involution on all $W$, acting as $-I$ on $U$.
It commutes with the lifted ideal and every complementary ideal,
hence with connected $J$, which is generated by exponentials.

Decompose $W=W_+\oplus W_-$ under $\tau$. Since $U\subset W_-$,
the minus part is nonzero. The plus part is also nonzero by the
actual $-I$ test. Projection compression gives positive constant
pure radii $r_+,r_-$ for both parts. For $p=b+x_++x_-$ consider
the actual $F=F(p,\tau p)$. It is $\tau$-invariant and its
unique affine center is $\tau$-fixed. Projection of its direction
$L_F$ to $U$ is nonzero, since the projected endpoint difference
is $2\Pi_U(p-b)\ne0$. Lemma 101.1 makes this projection injective.
But $(\tau+I)L_F\subset L_F$ projects to zero in $U$, so
$L_F\subset W_-$. Therefore

$$
c_F=b+x_+,\qquad p,\tau p\text{ are antipodes},\qquad
\rho_F=r_-.
\tag{102.1}
$$

The entire pure projection fiber over $x_+$ is exactly $\partial F$.
Indeed any other pure point with this plus projection gives a face
with the same center $b+x_+$. Two faces having that common relative
interior point contain each other, by the face property, so agree.
Conversely the whole boundary of $F$ has this projection. This also
identifies the whole $K$ fiber and proves its exposure. For
$w=x_+$, the original linear effect

$$
e_w(y)=\frac{u(y)}2+
\frac{Q_0(w,\Pi_+(y-u(y)b))}{2r_+^2}
\tag{102.2}
$$

is in $[0,u]$. Every point of $\Pi_+(K-b)$ lies in the closed
radius-$r_+$ ball. Equality $e_w(y)=1$ on $K$ holds exactly when
$\Pi_+(y-b)=w$, by Cauchy–Schwarz and $|w|=r_+$.
In a finite pure decomposition of such a $y$, every positive-weight
term also has effect value one and hence lies in $\partial F$.
Consequently

$$
K\cap\{\Pi_+(y-b)=w\}=K\cap\{e_w=1\}=F.
\tag{102.3}
$$

Only these derived central faces have been proved exposed.

The covariance is compatible with this same pure ensemble. The
$H$ orbit probability is $J$-invariant and is the unique invariant
probability on the transitive compact $J$-space $P$. Its mean and
covariance therefore equal the $J$ mean and covariance, namely the
original $b,S$. Since $\tau$ is central, $S$ is block diagonal.
For the actual face in (102.1), its auxiliary $J$-twirled moments
satisfy

$$
B_F^J=S\Pi_-,\qquad Z_F^J=S\Pi_+.
\tag{102.4}
$$

Indeed they twirl $x_-x_-^*$ and $x_+x_+^*$ respectively, which
are exactly the two covariance blocks. These are auxiliary moments
of the same ensemble; (100.2), defined by the original $H$ twirl,
has not been replaced by a new moment obstruction space.

Now the full fiber and transitivity, not the covariance identity
alone, supply isotropy. If two pures have plus projection $w$,
$J$ transitivity sends one to the other and equivariance forces
the sending element into $J_w$. Thus $J_w$ is transitive on the
entire $S^2$ fiber (102.3). Its identity component is transitive
too: its finitely many compact component orbits are open and closed
in that connected sphere. Projection to $U$ identifies the fiber
equivariantly with the whole sphere in the three-plane
$A=\Pi_UL_F\subset U$, centered at zero by (102.1).
The connected image preserves $A$ and fixes its normal line. A
proper connected compact subgroup of $SO(3)$ has algebra of
dimension at most one and cannot be sphere-transitive. Hence this
image contains the standard block $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ in
$\mathfrak{so}(4)$. Its complement is the irreducible standard
three-dimensional $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ module. Any strictly larger
subalgebra is therefore all $\mathfrak{so}(4)$; equality with the
block algebra would fix a line and contradict irreducibility of
$U$. Thus $\mathfrak k=\mathfrak{so}(4)$. Surjectivity on the
fiber's $SO(3)$, and injectivity of its projection to $U$, also
give its point stabilizer the corresponding $SO(2)$ rotations.
These are rotations of these actual faces, not arbitrary face
rotations granted as a premise.

Lift this full algebra as before to commuting axis ideals in
$\operatorname{Lie}J$, integrating to actual commuting
$\operatorname{SU}(2)_L,\operatorname{SU}(2)_R$ images. Both act
fundamentally on $U$. Put $N=U^\perp$. To rule out
$\dim N\le8$, the following elementary compact representation
facts are used inside this argument. In an irreducible complex
$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ module a highest-weight vector $v$ of weight
$a$ obeys

$$
EF^jv=j(a-j+1)F^{j-1}v.
\tag{102.5}
$$

The last nonzero element of this finite chain forces $a$ to be its
nonnegative integral length. The chain spans the irreducible module,
of dimension $a+1$, namely $V_a=\operatorname{Sym}^a\mathbb C^2$.
For two factors the irreducibles are $V_a\otimes V_c$, by decomposing
for one factor and using its multiplicity space for the other.
The standard equivariant antilinear map on $\mathbb C^2$ squares
to $-1$. On $V_a\otimes V_c$ its square is $(-1)^{a+c}$.
Schur's lemma fixes this sign up to a positive scalar. All these
modules are self-conjugate. Even $a+c$ gives a real form of real
dimension $(a+1)(c+1)$; odd $a+c$ gives quaternionic type and real
irreducible dimension $2(a+1)(c+1)$. To see that no smaller real
form is missed in the latter case, a real structure on $k$ complex
copies requires a multiplicity matrix $B$ with $B\overline B=-I$;
its determinant forces $k$ even. The symmetric powers integrate
these representations into the actual unit-quaternion group,
justifying also the integration used above.

On any $J$-irreducible real summand, restriction to either axis
ideal or their sum is real-isotypic. Its distinct isotypic components
would otherwise be preserved by that ideal and its commuting
complement, hence by connected $J$. If both factors act nontrivially
on an irreducible $N$ of dimension at most eight, both highest
weights are positive. Both even weights need at least $3\cdot3=9$
real dimensions; opposite parity needs at least $2\cdot2\cdot3=12$.
Thus both must be odd, and both central elements act as $-I$
throughout $N$, as they do on $U$. This contradicts the actual
$-I$ test. If one factor is trivial on $N$, that factor acts on
the whole $W$ as fundamental $U$ plus trivial modules, contradicting
the four-ball orbit test.

The cases are exhaustive. Dimension zero is excluded by the
$-I$ action on $U$; dimensions one through three by Theorem 101.2.
In dimensions four through eight, complete reducibility and the
minimum nonzero irreducible dimension four leave either irreducible
$N$, just treated, or exactly two four-dimensional irreducibles.
For one $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ the real irreducibles of dimension
at most four have dimensions $1,3,4$, the last fundamental.
Real-isotypy on a four-dimensional $J$ summand therefore permits
only trivial copies or one fundamental, not a three-plus-one
restriction. Thus in the two-summand case the whole $W$ restricts
to the left factor as fundamentals plus trivial modules, again
contradicting the actual four-ball test. Hence $\dim N\ge9$ and
$n\ge13$. ∎

**Corollary 102.2 (Original moment rank and nullity).** For
$0\ne D\in\mathcal M$, $D\succeq0$, rank four implies nullity
at least nine, and nullity four implies rank at least nine. At
$n=8$ or $9$, every such obstruction, if present, is full rank.

Proof. The range and kernel of the original $H$-invariant
self-adjoint $D$ are $J$-invariant in the same $W$. Apply Theorem

a nonzero singular obstruction has rank and nullity at least four.

singular witnesses with both dimensions at least five, and full-rank
witnesses, including scalar possibilities, remain unexcluded. The

**Proposition 102.3 (Averaging a supplied equalizer).** Existence of
an $H$-invariant equalizer is equivalent to existence of a
$G_{\rm full}=\operatorname{Aut}(C,u)$-invariant equalizer. Averaging
a supplied equalizer over $G_{\rm full}$ preserves its common radius.

Proof. Compactness of this full normalized group and its preservation

average is $G_{\rm full}$-invariant. The converse uses
$H\subset G_{\rm full}$. This preserves a supplied equalizer;
it does not construct one or supply legal full-group controls. ∎

## 104. The closed affine complex orbit and secant defect

**Definition 104.1 (Auxiliary algebraic carriers).** Complexify the
original $V$ and embed its original affine chart $u=1$ in
$\mathbb P(V_{\mathbb C})=\mathbb P^n_{\mathbb C}$. Let $Y$ be
the reduced complex affine Zariski closure of $P$, and $X$ its
reduced projective closure. Let $G_{\rm alg}$ be the actual
complex algebraic image of the complexification of $J=H^0$.
Use complex-bilinear extension in

$$
h(v)=Q_0(v-u(v)b,v-u(v)b)-R^2u(v)^2,
\qquad h(v)=\eta(v,v).
\tag{104.1}
$$

The real quadratic form $h$ has signature $(n,1)$. Denote the
projective secant variety by $SX$ and set
$\delta(X)=2m+1-\dim_{\mathbb C}SX$. None of these auxiliary
complex points or group elements is an additional original state
or legal control.

**Theorem 104.2 (Exact real locus and boundary).** One has
$Y=G_{\rm alg}p$, a closed smooth irreducible affine homogeneous
variety of complex dimension $m$, and $Y(\mathbb R)=P$.
The variety $X$ is irreducible, nondegenerate, of dimension $m$,
with

$$
X\cap\{u\ne0\}=Y,\qquad X(\mathbb R)=P,\qquad
\operatorname{Sing}X\subset X\cap\{u=0\}.
\tag{104.2}
$$

Every point at infinity is nonreal; projective smoothness is not
concluded.

Proof. Reuse $J$ transitivity from VI89.3. The compact real
algebraic action and reductive complexification are the compact-group
facts in [Schwarz §5][Schwarz]. They give Zariski density of $J$
in its connected complex algebraic image $G_{\rm alg}$. Extend
the invariant real product
$\langle tb+w,t'b+w'\rangle=tt'+Q_0(w,w')$ on $V$ to a
Hermitian product on $V_{\mathbb C}$. For
$A\in\operatorname{Lie}J$, both $Ap,p$ are real and $A$ is
skew-adjoint. Thus $\langle Ap,p\rangle=0$. The moment-map
formula $\mu(p)(A)=\langle Ap,p\rangle/(2i)$ vanishes.
The Kempf–Ness closed-orbit criterion in
[Losev, Theorem 4][Losev] now gives that $G_{\rm alg}p$ is closed.
It is not a replacement by a whole invariant-quotient fiber.

If a polynomial vanishes on $P=Jp$, its pullback along
$g\mapsto gp$ vanishes on Zariski-dense $J$ and hence on
$G_{\rm alg}$. Thus the closed orbit is exactly $Y$.
Connectedness gives irreducibility, and characteristic-zero
algebraic orbits are smooth. Its tangent at $p$ is
$(\operatorname{Lie}J\,p)\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C$, so
its complex dimension is the real orbit dimension $m$.

For a real affine $x\notin P$, the compact orbits $Jx$ and $P$
are disjoint. On their union, approximate the continuous function
zero on $P$ and one on $Jx$ by a real polynomial within $1/4$.
Haar-average it; averaging its finitely many coefficients still
gives a polynomial, invariant and constant on each orbit, with
distinct orbit values. Subtract its value on $P$. The resulting
polynomial vanishes on $P$ and not at $x$. Consequently
$Y(\mathbb R)=P$.

The homogeneous $h$ vanishes on the pure sphere and therefore on
$X$. A real projective point there with $u(v)=0$ would satisfy
$Q_0(v,v)=0$, impossible for $0\ne v\in W$. Closedness of $Y$
in the affine chart gives (104.2); smoothness of that chart places
all possible singularities at infinity. Finally
$\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}P=V$ gives projective
nondegeneracy. The common pure-orbit radius in (104.1) has not
equalized the different face radii. ∎

 \times(\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}),\qquad

of dimension $2m+1$, the positive real source

Its maximum rank is exactly $2m-1$. Therefore
$\bigwedge^{2m}d\beta$ vanishes identically by density, whereas
$\bigwedge^{2m-1}d\beta$ is nonzero at a supplied maximum-rank
point. In characteristic zero the generic differential rank equals
the dimension of the image closure: the function-field rank is
the transcendence degree, by separability and differentials.
Secants from the dense affine chart, with the two endpoint values
of the parameter deleted, are dense in $SX$. Its dimension is
therefore $2m-1$. The same density argument shows that the positive
binary image is Zariski dense in $SX$. This uses maximum real
rank, not constant rank everywhere or a global fiber bundle. ∎

## 105. Actual face quadrics and general complex pairs

**Proposition 105.1 (Every actual face quadric).** For every original
pair face $F$, put $A_F=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}F$.
Its dimension is four, and its whole boundary complexifies to the
smooth irreducible quadric surface

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_F&=\{[v]\in\mathbb P((A_F)_{\mathbb C}):f_F(v)=0\}
 \subset X,\\
f_F(v)&=Q_0(v-u(v)c_F,v-u(v)c_F)-\rho_F^2u(v)^2
 =h|_{A_F}(v).
\end{aligned}
\tag{105.1}
$$

Proof. Since $u(c_F)=1$, $u(L_F)=0$, one has
$A_F=\mathbb Rc_F\oplus L_F$. The equation in these coordinates
is a positive three-dimensional form minus $\rho_F^2$ times
the square of the first coordinate, so has rank four. Its real
sphere consists entirely of original pures and is Zariski dense
in the complex quadric: at a real smooth point its local real
form has the full real dimension two, and the holomorphic identity
principle applies. Thus every equation of $X$ vanishes on $Q_F$.
The orthogonality and radius identity in (97.2) give
$h|_{A_F}=f_F$ by direct expansion. ∎

**Theorem 105.2 (A genuine general-pair transfer).** A nonempty
complex Zariski open in $(X\times X)\setminus\Delta$ consists
of pairs lying on a smooth quadric surface contained in $X$.
Together with Theorem 104.3 this makes $X$ an LQEL-variety of
type two in [Russo, Definition 1.1][Russo]. It does not make $X$
an LQEL-manifold.

Proof. On $\operatorname{Gr}(4,V_{\mathbb C})$ let $\mathcal S$
be the tautological rank-four bundle. In
$\mathbb P(\operatorname{Sym}^2\mathcal S^*)$ take the open
of nondegenerate quadratic forms. Containment of their quadrics
in $X$ is a finite-type condition. In fact, choose finitely many
homogeneous generators $g_i$ of $I(X)$. For a generator of degree
$d_i\ge2$, divisibility of $g_i|_A$ by $f$ is the vanishing
of its class in the quotient of the constant-rank injective
multiplication map

$$
\operatorname{Sym}^{d_i-2}\mathcal S^*\otimes\mathcal O(-1)
\xrightarrow{\ \cdot f\ }
\operatorname{Sym}^{d_i}\mathcal S^*.
\tag{105.2}
$$

These are closed conditions on the open parameter space; degree-one
restrictions must be zero. The homogeneous ideal of a smooth
quadric hypersurface is generated by its quadratic equation, so
the conditions are also sufficient.

Incidence with two distinct points on each allowed quadric gives
a finite-type morphism to $(X\times X)\setminus\Delta$.
[Chevalley's theorem, Stacks tag 054K][Stacks] makes its image
constructible. By Proposition 105.1 it contains every distinct
original real pure pair, a dense subset. A dense constructible
subset of this irreducible Noetherian target contains a nonempty
open set. This proves the general-complex-pair assertion, not just
one real fiber. Theorem 104.3 gives the required quadric dimension
$\delta=2$, so Russo's equivalent general-pair definition applies.
No proper parameter map, smooth real face family or projective
smoothness has been assumed. ∎

**Proposition 105.3 (The real-visible entry component).** For every
positive binary point $z=tp+(1-t)q$, $p\ne q$, $0<t<1$, its
actual minimal face $F_z$ is $F(p,q)$. Its positive decomposition
first endpoints fill $\partial F_z$, and $Q_{F_z}$ is contained
in the reduced ordinary complex entry locus $\Sigma_z(X)$.
On the generic equidimensional entry open it is a component.

Proof. VI91.3 gives the face, its relative interior point $z$,
and all its positive binary decompositions, with first-endpoint
space the whole $S^2$. Their complex closure is $Q_{F_z}$.
For a general point on this quadric, the line through $z$ meets
it in a second distinct point, since $z$ is inside its span and
outside its equation. Hence all of $Q_{F_z}$ is in $\Sigma_z$.
The preliminary entry-dimension statement in [Russo §1][Russo]
gives a nonempty open in $SX\setminus X$ with reduced entry
loci equidimensional of dimension $\delta=2$. A closed irreducible
surface there is a component. The positive binary image is dense
by Theorem 104.3, so it meets that open. This is a visible-component
statement only; identifying every component requires §106. ∎

## 106. Full real entries and odd generic component count

**Definition 106.1 (Ordinary entries, reflection and incidence).** Set

$$
S_2^+=\{tp+(1-t)q:p\ne q\in P,\ 0<t<1\}.
\tag{106.1}
$$

For $z\in SX\setminus X$, let $\Sigma_z$ be the reduced Zariski
closure of all ordinary complex secant endpoints: distinct
$x,y\in X$ with $z$ on their projective line and different from
both endpoints. For $h(z)\ne0$ define the linear involution

$$
R_z(v)=v-\frac{2\eta(v,z)}{h(z)}z.
\tag{106.2}
$$

Let $T_z$ be the set of four-dimensional complex subspaces
$A\subset V_{\mathbb C}$ with $z\in\mathbb P(A)$,
$h|_A$ nondegenerate and
$Q_A=\mathbb P(A)\cap\{h=0\}\subset X$. Its cardinality counts
distinct geometric Grassmannian points, not scheme length.

**Theorem 106.2 (Every positive point has its full real entry
sphere).** For every $z\in S_2^+$,

$$
\Sigma_z(\mathbb R)=P\cap F_z=\partial F_z.
\tag{106.3}
$$

All real projective points are normalized by the original $u=1$.
The assertion includes real closure and tangent-limit points, not
only the positive binary fiber.

Proof. If $z=tp+(1-t)q$, direct polarization gives

$$
h(z)=-t(1-t)Q_0(p-q,p-q)<0.
\tag{106.4}
$$

For every ordinary secant through $z$, reflection $R_z$ preserves
$h$ and exchanges its two distinct null endpoints. Thus it sends
every ordinary endpoint into $X$. Since $X$ is closed and $R_z$
is a projective automorphism, it sends all of $\Sigma_z$ into
$X$. Its fixed null divisor is $\eta(v,z)=0$. The real form $h$
has index one and $z$ is timelike, so $z^\perp$ is positive
definite and this divisor has no real projective point.

Every real $x\in\Sigma_z$ is in $X(\mathbb R)=P$ and has
a distinct real partner $y=R_z[x]\in X(\mathbb R)=P$.
Neither can be at infinity, by Theorem 104.2. In the affine chart
the point $z$ is strictly inside the common pure sphere. The real
line through $x,y$ intersects that sphere in exactly these endpoints,
and $z$ lies strictly between them. The actual face property
therefore puts both in $F_z$. Conversely each boundary point of
the whole ball $F_z$ has a distinct boundary partner along the
chord through its interior point $z$, so it is an ordinary endpoint.
The pure points of an actual ball face are precisely its boundary.
This proves (106.3) for every $z$ in (106.1), including points
arising as complex-closure limits, real tangent limits or from
negative-weight presentations. It has not replaced the complex
entry locus by the positive real fiber. ∎

**Theorem 106.3 (Every generic component is a smooth quadric).**
There exists a conjugation-stable nonempty complex Zariski open
$\mathcal O\subset SX\cap\{u\ne0,h\ne0\}$ such that every
irreducible component of $\Sigma_z$, for every $z\in\mathcal O$,
is a smooth quadric surface $Q_A$. The correspondence

$$
T_z\longleftrightarrow\operatorname{Irr}(\Sigma_z),\qquad
A\longmapsto Q_A
\tag{106.5}
$$

is a bijection, and its finite cardinality $k$ is constant on
$\mathcal O$. For every positive real $z\in\mathcal O$ the
complex reduced $\Sigma_z$ is smooth at every real entry point.

Proof. First restrict to the generic equidimensional entry open
of Proposition 105.3 and to the smooth locus of the affine secant
variety. On the source $\mathcal D$ of (104.3), over this smooth
target of dimension $2m-1$, remove the images of rank-deficient
points as follows. The rank-deficient locus has finitely many
irreducible components. None can dominate the target: on the smooth
locus of a dominating component, characteristic-zero separability
would make the differential of its restricted map surjective at
a general point, contradicting rank deficiency of $d\beta$.
Their image closures are therefore proper. Deleting these closures
makes the entire remaining $\beta$ fibers smooth of dimension two.

Here is the exact comparison with the reduced entry locus. For
$u(x)=u(z)=1$ put

$$
\kappa(x,z)=\frac{2\eta(x,z)}{h(z)},\quad a_0=1-\kappa,
\qquad y=\frac{R_zx}{a_0},\quad t=\frac1\kappa.
\tag{106.6}
$$

On the open $\kappa a_0\ne0$, these regular formulas and
first-endpoint projection are inverse morphisms between the
$\beta$ fiber and that open of $\Sigma_z$. Indeed
$R_z(\Sigma_z)\subset X$ by the ordinary-endpoint reflection
and closure argument, and (106.6) gives distinct affine endpoints
with $z=tx+(1-t)y$, $t\notin\{0,1\}$. Conversely the reflection
uniquely recovers the partner of any such secant. For positive
real $z$, every real entry point lies in this open: the partner
is real, distinct and affine by Theorem 106.2. Thus the smooth
fiber statement gives complex smoothness at every real entry
point. No identity of schemes with a secant-cone intersection is
assumed.

To cover every generic component, take the ordinary ordered-secant
incidence, an open $\mathbb P^1$ bundle over
$(X\times X)\setminus\Delta$, of dimension $2m+1$. Choose the
nonempty good-pair open of Theorem 105.2, intersected with
$Y\times Y$. Bad pairs, either at infinity or outside this open,
have dimension at most $2m-1$, so their secant incidence has
dimension at most $2m$. Images of nondominating components can
be removed from $SX$. The dominating bad-incidence components
have generic fibers of dimension at most one over the target of
dimension $2m-1$; shrink again to ensure that bound.

For fixed $h(z)\ne0$, the first ordinary endpoint determines its
partner uniquely as $R_z[x]$. Ordinary endpoints are constructible
and dense in $\Sigma_z$ by its definition as their reduced
closure. They meet every two-dimensional component densely; the
unique partner prevents a hidden positive-dimensional fiber of
endpoint projection. The bad endpoints have dimension at most one,
so cannot cover any component. In each component choose a good
ordinary endpoint $x$ outside the other components, with partner
$y$. The smooth quadric $Q\subset X$ through this good pair has
$z$ in its three-dimensional projective span and outside $Q$.
Its secants through $z$ give $Q\subset\Sigma_z$. Being an
irreducible closed surface through $x$, it equals the chosen
component.

If $A$ is its four-dimensional span, $h|_A$ vanishes on $Q$ and
is nonzero because $h(z)\ne0$. It is a nonzero scalar multiple
of the defining rank-four quadratic equation of $Q$. Thus
$Q=Q_A$ and $h|_A$ is nondegenerate. Conversely every $A\in T_z$
gives such a quadric in $\Sigma_z$, hence a component by
equidimensionality. Its span recovers $A$, proving (106.5) and
finiteness of the set $T_z$.

For constancy, construct the incidence over $SX$ on the Grassmannian
open where $h|_A$ is nondegenerate, adding $z\in\mathbb P(A)$
and $h(z)\ne0$. Containment $Q_A\subset X$ is given by the
same finite ideal-generator divisibility and injective bundle maps
as (105.2), now with $f=h|_A$. This is a finite-type scheme over
$SX$. On the chosen open its fibers are finite as sets. Apply
[Stacks, Lemma 37.27.6, direct tag 055A][Stacks] at the generic
point of the irreducible base. It gives a further nonempty open
with constant number of geometric irreducible components of its
fibers. A zero-dimensional complex finite-type fiber has one such
component for each distinct geometric point, regardless of
nilpotents. The count is $|T_z|$, not length. Neither properness
nor flatness was required. Intersect the finitely many opens and
their conjugates; irreducibility preserves nonemptiness. Denote
the result by $\mathcal O$. ∎

**Theorem 106.4 (One real component and odd extras).** For every
$z\in\mathcal O\cap S_2^+$, the unique conjugation-fixed entry
component is $Q_{F_z}$. Every extra component has no real point
and occurs with a distinct conjugate. Hence the constant count is

$$
k=|T_z|=1+2a,\qquad a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.
\tag{106.7}
$$

The intersection $\mathcal O\cap S_2^+$ is nonempty and Zariski
dense. Conditional on an independently supplied generic bound
$|T_z|\le2$, one would obtain $k=1$ and QEL type two.

Proof. A conjugation-fixed component has conjugation-fixed span
$A=(A_{\mathbb R})_{\mathbb C}$ of real dimension four. It
contains the timelike real $z$, so $h|_{A_{\mathbb R}}$ has
signature $(3,1)$. In $u=1$ its real null locus is an entire round
$S^2$, bounding a three-ball $B_A$ with $z$ in its relative
interior. By $X(\mathbb R)=P$ this whole sphere is pure, and
$B_A\subset K$. Segment extension through $z$ and the actual
face property give $B_A\subset F_z$. Both balls have dimension
three and the entire boundary of $B_A$ lies in
$P\cap F_z=\partial F_z$. In the same affine three-space,
their sphere equations then agree, so $B_A=F_z$ and
$Q_A=Q_{F_z}$. The latter component exists by Proposition 105.3
and is fixed, so it is the unique fixed one.

If another component had a real point, (106.3) would place that
point also on $Q_{F_z}$. A reduced variety with two distinct
components through a point cannot be smooth there. Theorem 106.3
excludes this at every real entry point. The remaining components
are therefore nonreal conjugate pairs, proving (106.7).
Zariski density of $S_2^+$ from Theorem 104.3 gives the dense
nonempty intersection with $\mathcal O$. An additional generic
upper bound two, together with oddness, would force one component;
by Theorem 106.3 that whole general entry locus would be a smooth
quadric surface, which is Russo's QEL type-two condition.
Such an upper bound has not been proved here. Parity does not
exclude $k=3,5,\ldots$, prove projective smoothness at nonreal
infinity, or give an equalizer or a PSD classification. ∎

## 107. Controls on the algebraic implications

**Proposition 107.1 (Smooth affine orbit does not imply smooth
projective closure).** The weight-$(1,3)$ compact orbit

$$
a(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos3\theta,\sin3\theta)
\in\mathbb R^4
\tag{107.1}
$$

has a smooth closed affine complexification and two nonreal cusps
at projective infinity. Its whole convex hull is not an L3 body.
Thus this example blocks an affine-to-projective smoothness
shortcut, without being a countermodel to Definition 97.1.

Proof. The first two coordinates embed the real circle. After
an invertible complex linear change the affine orbit is
$(z,z^{-1},z^3,z^{-3})$, $z\in\mathbb C^*$, closed and
isomorphic to $\mathbb C^*$. The projective parametrization is
$[1:z:z^{-1}:z^3:z^{-3}]$. At $z=0$ it becomes

$$
[z^3:z^4:z^2:z^6:1].
\tag{107.2}
$$

The local ring is
$\mathbb C[z^2,z^3]_{(z^2,z^3)}$, of dimension one and cotangent
dimension two, so it is a cusp. The real structure sends
$z$ to $1/\overline z$, exchanging this point with the other
point at infinity; neither is real. The orbit is on a common
Euclidean sphere and spans $\mathbb R^4$ (its four independent
trigonometric coordinates have no linear relation). Its hull is
centrally symmetric since $a(\theta+\pi)=-a(\theta)$, with
interior center zero. Each orbit point is exposed by its Euclidean
support functional: equality on the sphere forces that point.
An antipodal pure pair has midpoint zero, so its minimal face is
the entire four-dimensional hull, not a three-ball. ∎

**Proposition 107.2 (Even smooth QEL type two is insufficient for
the reduced matrix-embedding shortcut).** Smooth QEL type two,
$n=2m$ and a proper secant variety alone do not characterize the
full rank-one complex matrix embedding relevant to (97.3).

Proof. Use as an intermediate published example
[Russo, Example 2.5][Russo]: the Plücker
$G(1,5)\subset\mathbb P^{14}$ has dimension eight and QEL
type four. Two successive general hyperplane sections are smooth
and irreducible by Bertini. At each step Russo's Proposition
1.3(iii), whose manifold and positive-defect hypotheses hold,
reduces dimension and defect by one. The resulting
$Z\subset\mathbb P^{12}$ has

$$
\dim Z=6,\quad \delta(Z)=2,\quad
\dim SZ=2\cdot6+1-2=11<12,\quad 12=2\dim Z.
\tag{107.3}
$$

The full rank-one $4\times4$ matrix variety is the Segre
$\mathbb P^3\times\mathbb P^3$, spanning $\mathbb P^{15}$,
so this cannot be its full embedding. This application of the
published section facts is a control on the stated reduced
implication, not a new classification. No compact real affine
orbit, original cone or whole L3 realization is supplied for $Z$.
It is compatible with $k=1$ and therefore also shows why the
conditional $k=1$ conclusion of §106 would not itself finish
the original reconstruction. ∎

**Proposition 107.3 (Every-rank complex PSD entry control).** For
the whole complex PSD family, every $N\ge2$ and every original
two-dimensional complex support give $k=|T_z|=1$ for all positive
binary $z$, including the trace-zero complex points of the entry
quadric at infinity.

Proof. Take the whole $\operatorname{Herm}_N(\mathbb C)$ cone,
$u=\operatorname{Tr}$, $b=I/N$ and HS on traceless matrices.
Then $R^2=1-1/N$ and on the complexification $M_N(\mathbb C)$

$$
h(A)=\operatorname{Tr}(A^2)-(\operatorname{Tr}A)^2.
\tag{107.4}
$$

The projective pure closure is the rank-one Segre variety. A
positive binary $z$ has rank two on its actual two-support $S_0$,
with positive eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ and
$h(z)=-2\lambda_1\lambda_2<0$.
If it is a nontrivial sum of two distinct rank-one complex
endpoints, rank two forces the two column lines to span
$\operatorname{im}z$ and the two row lines to span its row space.
Thus both endpoints lie in the corresponding full $2\times2$
block. Conversely, on that block
$h(A)=-2\det A$. Its rank-one determinant quadric is smooth,
and every general point of it has a distinct partner on a secant
through $z$. Taking the closure gives exactly the entire quadric,
including trace-zero complex boundary points. It is the sole
component, and its unique four-dimensional span is the sole
element of $T_z$. For $N=2$ the block is all $M_2(\mathbb C)$
and this is the quadric in the whole $\mathbb P^3$.
This is an every-rank control, not a proof of an entry-count bound
for arbitrary L3 carriers. ∎
