## 5. Singular generalized pencils without an inverse of the Gram

**Theorem 5.1 (exact polynomial and multiplicities).** Let $C=C(\rho)$ be nonzero singular, choose a unit kernel vector $n$, and write $\operatorname{adj}C=\gamma nn^\dagger$ with $\gamma>0$. Then

$$
\det(C-\lambda B)=\lambda(d_1+d_2\lambda-\alpha\beta\lambda^2),\quad
d_1=-\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}C\,B)=-\gamma n^\dagger Bn\ge0,\quad
d_2=\alpha\beta C_{DD}-\beta C_{TT}-\alpha C_{SS}.
\tag{PG.24}
$$

If $n^\dagger Bn<0$, zero is simple, there is exactly one positive root

$$
\lambda_0=\frac{d_2+\sqrt{d_2^2+4\alpha\beta d_1}}{2\alpha\beta},
\tag{PG.25}
$$

and one negative root. If the specimen is on the real off-plane quadratic, zero is exactly double, $d_2<0$, the third root is negative, and there is no positive generalized root or triple zero.

Proof. Expand the determinant in the diagonal entries of $\lambda B$. The constant is zero, its linear derivative is $-\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}C B)$, its quadratic coefficient is the displayed $d_2$, and its cubic coefficient is $-\det B=-\alpha\beta$. The rank-two Gram makes $\gamma$ the positive product of its two positive eigenvalues. For $d_1>0$ the quadratic in brackets has negative leading coefficient and opposite-sign roots, proving (PG.25) and the multiplicities.

For the real off-plane quadratic, split coordinates $D$ and $N=(T,S)$, and put $A_N=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,\beta)$. The lower principal block $C_N$ is positive definite: otherwise a lower-plane zero-Rayleigh vector for $C\succeq0$ would be a second independent kernel vector. Let $a_0=C_N^{-1}c_{ND}$; the kernel is proportional to $(1,-a_0)$, and $a_0^TA_Na_0=1$. Only this positive lower block is inverted. For $\lambda\ge0$ the Schur function is

$$
f(\lambda)=c_{DD}-\lambda-c_{DN}(C_N+\lambda A_N)^{-1}c_{ND}.
$$

It has $f(0)=f'(0)=0$ and

$$
f''(0)=-2a_0^TA_NC_N^{-1}A_Na_0<0.
$$

The vector $a_0$ is nonzero. Since
$\det(C-\lambda B)=\det(C_N+\lambda A_N)f(\lambda)$,
$d_2=\det(C_N)f''(0)/2<0$, with exactly a double zero. The remaining root is $d_2/(\alpha\beta)<0$. No continuity of a resource value and no inverse of singular $C$ enters. $\square$

For a pure plane specimen, $C=A|D\rangle\langle D|\oplus C_N$ with $A>0$, $\det C_N=0$, and (PG.24) also has the exact factorization

$$
\det(C-\lambda B)=(A-\lambda)\lambda
 [\alpha C_{SS}+\beta C_{TT}+\alpha\beta\lambda].
\tag{PG.26}
$$

The bracket has positive constant even if one specimen coordinate vanishes. Thus the positive root is $A$, zero is simple, and the other root is negative. For a mixed plane specimen the Gram can instead be positive definite; Section 9 treats both cases without using the singular factorization.

**Lemma 5.2 (nonnegative eigenbranch degeneracies).** For every $C\succeq0$ and $\lambda>0$, $\lambda B-C$ is strictly negative on the coefficient $D^\perp$ plane. It therefore has at most one nonnegative eigenvalue, and any such eigenvalue is simple.

Proof. On that plane $\lambda z^\dagger Bz-z^\dagger Cz<0$ for $z\ne0$. A nonnegative spectral subspace of dimension two would intersect this two-dimensional plane, a contradiction. In particular multiplicity two is impossible. This argument permits repeated negative eigenvalues, $\alpha=\beta$, zero individual $T$ or $S$ components, and all relative phases. It isolates the top branch without requiring distinct complementary eigenvalues. $\square$

## 6. Direct exact real quadratic-singular evaluation

**Theorem 6.1 (spectral law at the real quadratic boundary).** Suppose $C$ comes from the off-plane real quadratic stratum of Theorem 4.2. For $\lambda>0$ let
$\mu(\lambda)=\lambda_{\max}(\lambda B-C)$ and choose its real unit eigenvector $v(\lambda)$ with $v_D>0$. There is exactly one $\lambda_* >0$ with

$$
v(\lambda_*)^TBv(\lambda_*)=k.
\tag{PG.27}
$$

Its $\mu_*=\mu(\lambda_*)$ is strictly positive. Put $v=v(\lambda_*)$, $P=vv^T$ and

$$
F(C)=k\lambda_*-\mu_*=v^TCv>0,\qquad
S_{\rm cert}=C-\lambda_*B+\mu_*P .
\tag{PG.28}
$$

Then $S_{\rm cert}\succeq0$ has rank two and kernel $\mathbb Rv$. Among all complex Hermitian $0\preceq H\preceq I$ with $\operatorname{tr}(BH)\ge k$, the minimum of $\operatorname{tr}(CH)$ is $F(C)$ and its unique minimizer is $P$.

Proof. The real kernel $n$ has $n^TBn=0$, so its Rayleigh value in $\lambda B-C$ is zero. If the top value were zero, the negative semidefinite matrix would kill $n$, whereas its product with $n$ is $\lambda Bn\ne0$. Thus $\mu(\lambda)>0$. Lemma 5.2 makes this eigenvalue simple, and its Rayleigh identity gives $b(\lambda):=v^TBv\ge\mu/\lambda>0$, so $v_D\ne0$ and its sign is well defined.

Differentiating a normalized simple eigenpair and projecting onto its orthogonal eigenvectors gives

$$
\mu'=b,\qquad
b'=2\sum_{j\ne{\rm top}}\frac{|v_j^TBv|^2}{\mu-\mu_j}>0 .
\tag{PG.29}
$$

Repeated negative eigenvalues cause no problem: the sum is the positive quadratic form of the inverse restricted spectral gap, independent of their chosen orthonormal basis. If it were zero, $Bv$ would be proportional to $v$. Positive $b$ forces $v=e_D$ up to sign, since all other eigenspaces of $B$ are negative. But $e_D$ cannot be an eigenvector of $C$: if $c_{ND}=0$, $Cn=0$ and $n_D\ne0$ would force $c_{DD}=0$, whereas $D$ is invertible and the specimen is nonzero. This proves strictness.

At $\lambda=0$ the simple zero eigenpair of $-C$ is its kernel. Local simple-eigenpair continuity, not continuity of an optimization value, gives $b(\lambda)\to0$. For large $\lambda$, the $e_D$ Rayleigh test yields
$\mu\ge\lambda-c_{DD}$, while $\mu=\lambda b-v^TCv\le\lambda b$. Hence $0\le1-b\le c_{DD}/\lambda$ and $b\to1$. Strict monotonicity and $0<k<1$ give the unique selector (PG.27).

On $v^\perp$, $C-\lambda_*B$ has the two strictly positive eigenvalues $-\mu_j$, while its eigenvalue on $v$ is $-\mu_*$. Adding $\mu_*P$ proves the claimed certificate and rank. For any complex feasible $H$,

$$
\operatorname{tr}(CH)-F(C)
 =\lambda_*[\operatorname{tr}(BH)-k]
   +\operatorname{tr}(S_{\rm cert}H)
   +\mu_*[1-v^\dagger Hv]\ge0.
\tag{PG.30}
$$

All terms are nonnegative. Equality forces $\operatorname{ran}H\subseteq\mathbb Cv$ and $v^\dagger Hv=1$, so $H=P$. This also supplies equality in the bound. Finally $v^TCv$ cannot vanish: the Gram kernel has $B$ value zero, while $v$ has positive $B$ value $k$. $\square$

## 7. The exact saturated equality theorem
