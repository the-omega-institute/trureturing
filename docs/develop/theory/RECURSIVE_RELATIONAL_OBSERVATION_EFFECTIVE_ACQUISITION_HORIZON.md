$$

V_A=\sup_{w\in\Gamma^*}p_A(w).
\tag{3.1}
$$

A row of such a matrix is deterministic or has two equally likely successors, so one fresh fair bit suffices for an update. Add a private proof letter $I$ with identity matrix. **Construction 3.2 (balanced rejected-pair code and finite checker).** Choose the least integer $t\ge1$ with $\binom{2t}{t}\ge|\Gamma|+1$. Assign each letter of $\Gamma\cup\{I\}$ a distinct length-$2t$ word over the two *tokens* $AA,BB$, with exactly $t$ tokens of each type. $$

2t\ \alpha\text{s and }2t\ \beta\text{s}.
\tag{3.2}
$$

At a seed-pair boundary the private checker retains a proper token prefix of the finite codebook (or its invalid flag) and one sampled $Q$ label initialized to $q_0$. The first letter of a pair stays only in the original half-pair control. Only when its second letter is equal is that rejected token committed to the code prefix; an unequal pair instead performs original seed acceptance. Thus even a codebook omitting a first-letter prefix never rejects a legal seed prematurely. At a completed recognized block it updates that label by its $M_x$, using a fresh fair bit if needed. Unknown complete blocks, noncode token prefixes and acceptance of a seed in a partial block make the additional validity flag permanently false. ## 7. Primary strict-cutpoint premise and an explicit promise-gap amplifier

**Mathematical reference 7.1 (verified external premise).** Gimbert and Oualhadj, *Probabilistic Automata on Finite Words: Decidable and Undecidable Problems*, author full version [GO], §1 Definition 1, §2 Problem 1 and Definition 2, Theorem 1 and its appendix proof, state that it is undecidable whether a finite simple automaton has a finite word with acceptance **strictly greater than $1/2$**. **Lemma 7.2 (finite simple amplifier with a strict fixed slack).** From a simple automaton $A_0$ one can effectively construct a simple automaton $A$ with

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\forall w:p_{A_0}(w)\le1/2&\Longrightarrow V_A\le1/2,\\
\exists w:p_{A_0}(w)>1/2&\Longrightarrow V_A=1.
\end{array}
\tag{7.1}
$$

No assertion of attainment of $V_A=1$ is made.

**Proof.** Extend the input alphabet by delimiters $\tau,\#$, disjoint from the old alphabet. Maintain a finite $A_0$ simulation label, a finite round flag in “no trial yet/all accepted/all rejected/mixed”, and a syntax flag recording whether the latest operation completed a trial. There are accepting and rejecting absorbing sinks; only the accepting sink is final. A trial is an old-alphabet word, possibly empty, followed by $\tau$. At $\tau$ inspect the accepting predicate of the currently sampled $A_0$ label, update the round flag by that Boolean result, and set that private simulation label to $q_0$ for the next trial. This is a deterministic finite automaton transition, not a source or PH operation. A $\#$ is legal only immediately after at least one completed trial. On $\#$ an all-accepted round enters the accepting sink, an all-rejected round the rejecting sink, and a mixed round returns to the initial unresolved mode with round flag cleared. Old letters simulate $A_0$ and clear the “just completed” syntax flag. A malformed $\#$ sends all remaining unresolved mass to the rejecting sink; already accepted mass stays accepted. Unfinished trials or rounds contribute no new acceptance. This is an explicit finite-state description; every transition is deterministic or a single fair split inherited from $A_0$, so it remains simple.

For one syntactically completed round with trial words $w_1,\ldots,w_n$, let $p_i=p_{A_0}(w_i)$. The fresh independent finite-state simulations give, relative to its incoming unresolved mass,

$$
G=\prod_{i=1}^np_i,\qquad D=\prod_{i=1}^n(1-p_i),
\tag{7.2}
$$

as the absorbing acceptance and rejection probabilities; all mixed outcomes remain unresolved. If every old word has $p_i\le1/2$, then $G\le D$. At each completed round the newly accepting mass is at most the newly rejecting mass. Summing these increments over any finite input gives total accepting mass at most total rejecting mass, and hence at most $1/2$. Malformed input only adds rejection, and unfinished input adds neither acceptance nor an exception to the inequality. This handles all finite inputs, not just repeated well-formed words.

If some old word has $p>1/2$, use $n$ copies of that trial per round, and $m$ identical completed rounds. Here $G=p^n$, $D=(1-p)^n$ and unresolved survival per round is $1-G-D$. Its finite-word acceptance is exactly

$$
\frac{p^n}{p^n+(1-p)^n}
\left[1-\left(1-p^n-(1-p)^n\right)^m\right].
\tag{7.3}
$$

For $p=1$ this is 1 after one round. For $1/2<p<1$, choose a finite $n$ making the first factor as close to 1 as required, since $((1-p)/p)^n\to0$. For that fixed $n$, $G+D>0$ and the second factor tends to 1 as $m\to\infty$. Thus arbitrarily close values below 1 occur on finite words, and $V_A=1$. No unbounded integer $n$ or $m$ is stored in the automaton; their repetition is part of the finite input word. The finite construction is computable and proves (7.1) using only the strict-cutpoint primary premise and this elementary argument. ∎
