# FIB strict-real joint incidence: the trace-corrected complex M projection curve

## 1. Complete worlds and the corrected complex incidence

**Definition 1.1 (Original clocks and complete worlds).** A strict-real world is
one complete triple $W=(P,g,d)$ with

$$
P=P(r,b)=\begin{pmatrix}r&b\\r(1-r)/b&1-r\end{pmatrix},\qquad
0<r<1,\quad b>0,\quad
0\ne g\in\mathbb R[s],\quad\deg g\le3,\quad d\in\mathbb R.
\tag{1.1}
$$

The two actions, left products, and original readings are

$$
\mathsf J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathsf M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q_0=I,\quad Q_i=E_iQ_{i-1},\qquad
y_i=d+g(i)\operatorname{tr}(Q_iP).
\tag{1.2}
$$

The acquired word through clock six is
$\mathsf J,\mathsf M,\mathsf J,\mathsf M,\mathsf M,\mathsf M$, and its
readings are

$$
(y_0,y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4,y_5,y_6)=(0,24,147/5,24,18,p,v).
\tag{1.3}
$$

Every acquired reading and each clock-seven alternative uses this same $P$,
entire polynomial $g$, and fixed drift $d$. Write $C(p,v)$ for the entire
strict-real child satisfying (1.3), with $p\ne24$. Lower gain degrees,
sampled zeros, repeated gain roots, zero drift, and singular parent sources
are included. Neither positive off-diagonal entry has an upper bound.

Set

$$
c=\frac{r(1-r)}b,\qquad j=b+c,\qquad n=j+r,\qquad
T=1+b,\qquad U=1+2b,\qquad e=d-24.
\tag{1.4}
$$

The terminal alternatives on this world are

$$
a(W)=d+(T+2j+4r)g(7),\qquad
m(W)=d+(3U+2n)g(7).
\tag{1.5}
$$

The actual pair images are

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_J&=\{(p,v):p\ne24,\ \exists W\ne W'\in C(p,v),\ a(W)=a(W')\},\\
D_M&=\{(p,v):p\ne24,\ \exists W\ne W'\in C(p,v),\ m(W)=m(W')\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.6}
$$

The joint predicate is membership in $D_J\cap D_M$. Its two pairs are
independently chosen in the same child; cross-pair reuse is allowed and the
two common terminal values may differ.

## 2. The original-source bridge and the restored clock

**Proposition 2.1 (Literal traces and the clock-four identity).** The traces
at clocks zero through six, and the two terminal traces, are respectively

$$
(1,j,T,n,U,U+n,2U+n),\qquad T+2j+4r,\qquad 3U+2n.
\tag{2.1}
$$

Suppress a label in (1.9), and set

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_0&=q_0+24+e,&\ell_1&=jq_1+e,\\
\ell_2&=5Tq_2-27+5e,&\ell_3&=nq_3+e,\\
C_4&=U(-q_0+4q_1-6q_2+4q_3)+6+e.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.2}
$$

With $T=1+b,U=1+2b$, one has the polynomial identity

$$
\begin{aligned}
5TjnC_4={}&P_{\mathrm{raw}}
-5TUjn\ell_0+20TUn\ell_1\\
&-6Ujn\ell_2+20TUj\ell_3.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.3}
$$

Consequently the $Q_M$-guarded raw equations recover the original reading
$y_4=18$ in both labels, and $C_4\in J_M$ for each label.

Proof. Multiplication in (1.2) gives

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
i&Q_i\\ \hline
0&I\\
1&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
2&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
3&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
4&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
5&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\3&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
6&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\5&2\end{smallmatrix}\right).
\end{array}
\tag{2.4}
$$

The terminal left products are
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\3&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}5&2\\8&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Taking their
traces against $P$ gives (2.1), including the terminal factors in (1.5).

The unique interpolant through $q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3$ at $0,1,2,3$ has
the values (1.8). These are the Lagrange extrapolation rows; see
Berrut–Trefethen, *Barycentric Lagrange Interpolation*, §2,
[doi:10.1137/S0036144502417715](https://doi.org/10.1137/S0036144502417715).
Thus $C_4$ is exactly $y_4-18$. To verify (2.3), expand its left
side and subtract its right side. The coefficients of
$q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,e$ and the constant coefficient all vanish,
using $T=1+b,U=1+2b$. This proves the identity without dividing
by $e$, a gain sample, or the parent coefficient $\Lambda$.

Its right side belongs to $I_M$. Since $Tjn$ divides $Q_M$ up to
the other polynomial factors, multiplication by a suitable factor gives
$Q_MC_4\in I_M$; the nonzero rational constant $5$ is a unit.
This proves the asserted saturation membership. If the auxiliary traces
are independent coordinates, the identity is read modulo their defining
equations, so its conclusion is identical. ∎

**Definition 2.2 (The all-source open surface).** In $\mathbb A^3_{b,r,e}$
set

$$
\begin{gathered}
J=b^2+r-r^2,\qquad N=J+br,\qquad
L=b^2+8b+4,\qquad A=(8b-1)(5b+2),\\
C=2LJN-4bTU(J+N),\qquad
\mathcal P=5eC+6AJN,
\end{gathered}
\tag{2.5}
$$

where $T=1+b,U=1+2b$. Here $J$ is a polynomial, distinct from the
action matrix $\mathsf J$. Define

$$
X=V_{\mathbb C}(\mathcal P)\cap
\{b\,r(1-r)\,T\,U\,J\,N\ne0\}.
\tag{2.6}
$$

On $X$ put $j=J/b,n=N/b,d=24+e$. The gain is the one polynomial
of degree at most three with samples

$$
(q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3)
=\left(-24-e,-\frac e j,\frac{27/5-e}{T},-\frac e n\right).
\tag{2.7}
$$

Define regular functions $p,v,m$ on $X$ by (1.8)–(1.9), and write
$\rho_X=(p,v):X\to\mathbb A^2$ for the acquired map. The letter
$\rho_X$ is distinct from the leading coefficient $\rho$ below.

**Proposition 2.3 (Bidirectional bridge on the whole source open).**
After forgetting the three slack signs, the one-world part of the
$Q_M$-guarded raw incidence is exactly $X$, with (2.7) and the literal
responses just defined. Each point of $X$ has exactly eight choices of its
three complex square slacks. Its gain is never identically zero.

Every strict-real world in the original parent belongs to this source
open, and its strict-real raw lift satisfies exactly the original readings.
Saturating by $Q_M$ preserves every real point of the raw system.

Proof. A raw complex point has $t,b,1-tb\ne0$ by its slack equations.
Putting $r=tb$ gives $b,r,1-r\ne0$ and
$j=b+r(1-r)/b=J/b$, $n=N/b$. The additional $Q_M$ guard gives
$T,U,J,N\ne0$. The four linear gain equations then force (2.7).
There is an exact identity

$$
\mathcal P=b^2P_{\mathrm{raw}}.
\tag{2.8}
$$

Hence the source lies in $X$. Proposition 2.1 restores the missing
clock-four equation. Equations (1.9) supply clocks five, six, and the
literal terminal $M$ response. All these values come from one entire
interpolant.

Conversely a point of $X$, with $t=r/b$, supplies (2.7), (2.8), and
all original readings by (2.3). It has slack choices given by

$$
\alpha^2=b/r,\qquad\beta^2=1/b,\qquad
\gamma^2=1/(1-r).
\tag{2.9}
$$

Each right side is nonzero, so over $\mathbb C$ each has precisely
two square roots, independently. If the gain were zero, clocks zero and
one would both equal the same drift, contradicting their readings $0,24$.
This also proves nonzeroness in every lower-degree case.

For a strict-real source, $t=r/b>0$, $b>0$, $1-r>0$, and

$$
T>1,\qquad U>1,\qquad j=b+c>0,\qquad n=j+r>0.
\tag{2.10}
$$

Thus the real slack equations are equivalent to these original strict
source inequalities and $Q_M>0$. Conversely every real raw point has
these inequalities because a real nonzero square is positive. For any
$f\in I_M:Q_M^\infty$, some $Q_M^kf$ belongs to $I_M$.
At a real raw point $Q_M>0$, so $f=0$. The inclusion
$I_M\subseteq I_M:Q_M^\infty$ proves the reverse inclusion of real
zero sets. This argument imposes no bound on drift or gain coefficients
and no separation floor on any two sources. ∎

**Proposition 2.4 (Strict-real pair distinctness).** In one strict-real
off-$24$ child, two complete worlds with the same source are equal.
Consequently (1.10) encodes exactly complete-world distinctness for the
strict-real $M$ pair.

Proof. This is the source-uniqueness consequence of O, Theorem 4.2,
with its original fifth response. For completeness put

$$
\Lambda=1+\frac6T+\frac1U-\frac4j-\frac4n.
\tag{2.11}
$$

The undivided parent equation is

$$
e\Lambda+\frac{24}{5}(b-1/8)(6/T-2/U)=0.
\tag{2.12}
$$

If $\Lambda\ne0$, the source determines $e$. If $\Lambda=0$,
strict positivity gives $6/T-2/U=2(2+5b)/(TU)>0$, so
$b=1/8$ and $1/j+1/n=107/60$. At this source the coefficient
of $e$ in the original fifth response is

$$
A_5=1+(n+5/4)(71/18-5/j).
\tag{2.13}
$$

Since $n>j>0$, one has $j<120/107<n$ and
$71/18-5/j<-37/72$. Therefore

$$
A_5<1-\frac{37}{72}\left(\frac{120}{107}+\frac54\right)
=-\frac{6739}{30816}<0.
\tag{2.14}
$$

The common $p$ determines $e$ also on this singular stratum. The four
samples (2.7) now determine the entire gain and the drift. Equal sources
therefore mean equal complete triples. A real squared source distance is
positive exactly when the sources differ, so its inverse exists precisely
for a distinct pair. ∎

## 3. Absolute irreducibility and the retained exceptional images

**Theorem 3.1 (Absolute irreducibility of the original-source parent).**
The polynomial $\mathcal P=5eC+6AJN$ is absolutely irreducible.
Consequently $X$ is a nonempty irreducible surface. The acquired map
$\rho_X$ is dominant.

Proof. Work first in $\mathbb C[b,r]$, a unique factorization domain.
A common factor of $J,N$ divides $N-J=br$. Neither $b$ nor $r$
divides $J$, so $\gcd(J,N)=1$. Substitution of $b=0,-1,-1/2$
shows that neither $J$ nor $N$ has a common factor with $bTU$.
Modulo $J$ and modulo $N$, respectively,

$$
C\equiv-4bTUN\pmod J,\qquad
C\equiv-4bTUJ\pmod N.
\tag{3.1}
$$

These identities exclude all common factors of $C$ with $JN$.
The remaining factors of $A$ are excluded by the exact specializations

$$
\begin{aligned}
C(1/8,r)
&=\frac{3(438272r^4-931328r^3+540800r^2-50728r-853)}{131072},\\
C(-2/5,r)
&=\frac{48(625r^4-1000r^3+50r^2+260r+36)}{15625}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.2}
$$

Both are nonzero polynomials. Thus $5C$ and $6AJN$ are coprime
over $\mathbb C$. Over $\mathbb Z[b,r]$, the corresponding gcd of
$C$ and $6AJN$ is the scalar $2$; scalar content is irrelevant over
$\mathbb C$. The polynomial $\mathcal P$ is primitive in $e$ and
linear with nonzero coefficient. It is irreducible over
$\mathbb C(b,r)[e]$, hence over $\mathbb C[b,r,e]$ by Gauss' lemma.
This proves absolute irreducibility without assuming that any particular
gain coefficient or sample is nonzero.

At $r=1/2,b=1$ the parent has

$$
\begin{gathered}
j=5/4,\quad n=7/4,\quad T=2,\quad U=3,\quad
\Lambda=-121/105,\quad e=1029/121,\\
p=9918/121,\quad v=1068459/2420,\quad m=206823/121.
\end{gathered}
\tag{3.3}
$$

All factors defining $X$ are nonzero. In the local chart
$e=-6AJN/(5C)$, differentiation of (2.7) and (1.8) gives

$$
\det\frac{\partial(p,v)}{\partial(r,b)}
=-\frac{1550712402}{805255}\ne0
\tag{3.4}
$$

at this point. The map therefore has a two-dimensional local image and
is dominant. This is an application of the rank criterion for a morphism
in characteristic zero; equivalently its two coordinate functions are
algebraically independent. The irreducible hypersurface has dimension two,
and its nonempty open $X$ has the same dimension. ∎

**Proposition 3.2 (Four exceptional source sets have proper image).** Let

$$
E=X\cap\bigl(
\{d=0\}\cup\{e=0\}\cup\{n+U=0\}\cup\{n+2U=0\}
\bigr),\qquad
Z_E=\overline{\rho_X(E)}^{\mathrm{Zar}}.
\tag{3.5}
$$

Each of the four source sets has dimension at most one, and
$\dim Z_E\le1$. In particular $Z_E$ is a proper closed subset of
the base plane. The singular source set $X\cap\{\Lambda=0\}$
also has acquired image closure of dimension at most one. These
subsets remain part of $X$; (3.5) does not localize $X$ by any
of their defining equations.

Proof. None of the four functions is identically zero on $X$.
For $e$ and $d$ this follows already from (3.3); algebraically
$\mathcal P|_{e=0}=6AJN$ and $\mathcal P|_{e=-24}$ are nonzero.
The last two functions have polynomial numerators

$$
N+bU,\qquad N+2bU,
\tag{3.6}
$$

which are nonzero and independent of $e$. The irreducible polynomial
$\mathcal P$ of $e$-degree one cannot divide either numerator.
On an irreducible surface the zero set of a nonzero regular function
has dimension at most one. The response map is regular on $X$, so
the closure of its image of each such subset has dimension at most one.
The finite union has that same bound. These standard dimension facts
are used here only for the four explicitly specified source functions;
see Hartshorne, *Algebraic Geometry*, Chapter I, for dimension and
images of morphisms.

For the singular set, $\Lambda=C/(TUJN)$ on $X$. If $C=0$,
the parent equation and $JN\ne0$ give $A=0$, hence
$b=1/8$ or $b=-2/5$. At either value, (3.2) restricts $r$ to
a finite set, while $e$ is unrestricted by the parent equation.
Thus the singular set is contained in a finite union of lines and
has dimension at most one, as does its acquired image closure.
There is no division by $\Lambda$ in this argument. ∎

## 4. The fixed finite carrier and its drift equation

**Definition 4.1 (Interpolation from clocks three through six).** In
$\mathbb Z[x,d,p,v]$ put

$$
\begin{gathered}
D=x(x+1)(x+2),\\
z_3=(24-d)(x+1)(x+2),\qquad z_4=(18-d)D,\\
z_5=(p-d)x(x+2),\qquad z_6=(v-d)x(x+1),\\
a_0=20z_3-45z_4+36z_5-10z_6,\\
a_1=10z_3-20z_4+15z_5-4z_6,\\
a_2=4z_3-6z_4+4z_5-z_6,\\
a_7=-z_3+4z_4-6z_5+4z_6,\qquad
z_*=xa_1-D(24-d).
\end{gathered}
\tag{4.1}
$$

For $D\ne0$, $H$ denotes the degree-at-most-three interpolant at
$3,4,5,6$ with samples

$$
\left(\frac{24-d}{x},\ 18-d,\
\frac{p-d}{x+1},\ \frac{v-d}{x+2}\right).
\tag{4.2}
$$

Its values $H_i=H(i)$, for $i=0,1,2,7$, equal $a_i/D$.
Define the integer polynomials

$$
\begin{aligned}
f={}&5dDa_2+a_0\bigl((294-10d)D-5a_2\bigr),\\
K={}&4a_0^2z_*^2+2a_0(dD-a_0)z_*a_1\\
&-(a_0+dD)\bigl(dD+(1-2x)a_0\bigr)a_1^2,\\
F_0={}&405x^3+(975-18p+5v)x^2+(90-36p+5v)x-480.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.3}
$$

These are exactly the F, Definition 31.1, forms, with the normalization
$K=4k$. The $d$-degrees are two and four. Write

$$
f=ad^2+b_fd+c_f,\qquad K=A_4d^4+A_3d^3+A_2d^2+A_1d+A_0
\tag{4.4}
$$

and define

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha={}&A_4(-b_f^3+2ab_fc_f)
+A_3a(b_f^2-ac_f)-A_2a^2b_f+A_1a^3,\\
\beta={}&A_4(-b_f^2c_f+ac_f^2)
+A_3ab_fc_f-A_2a^2c_f+A_0a^3.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.5}
$$

The symbols $\alpha,\beta$ in (4.5) are polynomials on this chart,
not the source square slacks of Section 1.

**Proposition 4.2 (Exact normalization and pseudo-remainder).** One has

$$
f\mid a^3K-(\alpha d+\beta)
\quad\hbox{in }\mathbb Z[x,p,v,d],
\tag{4.6}
$$

and the fixed degree-$(2,4)$ resultant satisfies

$$
\operatorname{Res}_d(f,K)
=\frac{a\beta^2-b_f\alpha\beta+c_f\alpha^2}{a^3}
=256D^8F_0^2R.
\tag{4.7}
$$

Here $R\in\mathbb Z[x,p,v]$ is the uniquely normalized F carrier:
it has $512$ nonzero monomials and coordinate degrees
$(\deg_xR,\deg_pR,\deg_vR)=(18,6,6)$. Both quotients in (4.7)
are exact polynomial quotients. Thus (4.1), (4.3)–(4.5), and (4.7)
specify every integer coefficient of $R$ without a coefficient list.
Furthermore

$$
a_0(x,0)=-2F_0,
\qquad F_0\mid\beta,\quad
F_0\mid f(x,0),\quad F_0\mid K(x,0).
\tag{4.8}
$$

Proof. Reduction of $d^2,d^3,d^4$ using
$ad^2=-b_fd-c_f$ gives (4.5) after multiplication by $a^3$.
This proves (4.6) as a polynomial identity, including where $a=0$.
Over $\mathbb Q(x,p,v)$, evaluation at the two roots of $f$ gives
the first equality of (4.7). Equivalently the resultant of the linear
remainder is $a\beta^2-b_f\alpha\beta+c_f\alpha^2$, and retaining
degree four for the second polynomial introduces the factor $a^3$.
This also derives the formula if a particular $K$ loses degree.

For the second equality, F, Lemma 31.2, gives
$\operatorname{Res}_d(f,k)=16D^8F_0^2R$. Scaling the second
polynomial by $4$ scales the resultant by $4^{\deg_df}=16$.
Thus $K=4k$ gives the factor $256$, not $16$. The exact F
division fixes the degrees and normalization asserted here. Direct
substitution of $d=0$ in (4.1) proves the first identity of (4.8).
Then $f(x,0)$ and $K(x,0)$ are divisible by $F_0$, while
the expression for $\beta$ shows the same divisibility for $\beta$.
These are existing carrier identities used in the source reconstruction,
not a new interpretation of their roots as actual worlds. ∎

**Proposition 4.3 (Forward equations and the retained $F_0$ branch).**
For a source in $X\setminus E$, set $x=n/U$ and $H=Ug$.
Then $D\ne0$, $d\ne0$, $e\ne0$, and this is precisely the
interpolant (4.2). It satisfies $f=K=0$. Let

$$
S_F(p,v)=\operatorname{Res}_x(F_0,\alpha).
\tag{4.9}
$$

At any base with $S_F\ne0$, such a source has $R(x,p,v)=0$.
Thus the $F_0$ factor in (4.7) does not supply an additional generic
nonzero-drift source branch. More explicitly, the closure of the
acquired image of the entire source subset $F_0(n/U,p,v)=0$ is
contained in $Z_E\cup V(S_F)$ and has dimension at most one.

Proof. The conditions defining $X\setminus E$ give $U,n,n+U,n+2U\ne0$,
so $x,x+1,x+2$ are nonzero. The actual clocks three through six,
with the traces (2.1), give exactly (4.2). Put

$$
B_0=294/5-2d-H_2.
\tag{4.10}
$$

Clocks zero and two imply $dU=-H_0$ and $B_0U=H_2$.
Consequently $dH_2+H_0B_0=0$, which is $f=0$ after
multiplication by $5D^2$. Clock one gives
$H_1=U(24-d)/j\ne0$.

The rank-one source relation in these coordinates is

$$
r^2-(1+b)r+b(n-b)=0.
\tag{4.11}
$$

With $Z=xH_1-(24-d)$, the actual source has
$U=-H_0/d$, $b=(U-1)/2$, and $r=UZ/H_1$.
Substitution in (4.11) and multiplication by $4d^2H_1^2$
gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
4H_0^2Z^2+2H_0(d-H_0)ZH_1
-(H_0+d)\bigl(d+(1-2x)H_0\bigr)H_1^2=0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.12}
$$

Its $D^4$ multiple is $K=0$. The resultant now implies $F_0R=0$.

If $F_0=0$ and $S_F\ne0$, then $\alpha\ne0$ at that $x$.
By (4.8), $d=0$ is a common root of $f,K$ there and $\beta=0$.
Every common root satisfies $\alpha d+\beta=0$ by (4.6), so
$d=0$ is the only common root. It cannot represent the source under
consideration, for which $d\ne0$. Therefore $R=0$.

Every source with $F_0=0$ is either in $E$, whose image is in
$Z_E$, or is covered by this argument and has base in $V(S_F)$.
Equation (5.8) below proves that $S_F$ is a nonzero polynomial.
Both closed image bounds therefore have dimension at most one.

This argument uses $S_F\ne0$ only on a base open. On $S_F=0$,
or on the source subset $d=0$, the $F_0$ branch is retained. No
global saturation by $F_0$ or by drift has occurred. ∎

## 5. Exact finite algebra and the terminal certificate

**Definition 5.1 (The carrier algebra and source functions).** Put
$k=\mathbb Q(p,v)$ and $\rho=[x^{18}]R$. In the degree-eighteen
algebra

$$
B=k[x]/(\bar R),\qquad \bar R=R/\rho,
\tag{5.1}
$$

define, whenever the indicated elements are units,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\delta=-\beta/\alpha,\qquad H_i=a_i(x,\delta)/D\quad(i=0,1,2,7),\\
U=-H_0/\delta,\qquad b=(U-1)/2,\qquad
j=\frac{U(24-\delta)}{H_1},\qquad n=xU,\qquad r=n-j,\quad T=1+b,\\
\tau=\delta+(2x+3)H_7.
\end{gathered}
\tag{5.2}
$$

All representatives have degree less than eighteen in $x$. For a
representative $u$, its multiplication matrix $M(u)$ has column $j$
equal to the coefficient vector of
$\operatorname{rem}_x(ux^j,\bar R)$, $0\le j<18$. Define

$$
\operatorname{Nm}(u)=\det M(u),\qquad
\chi(z)=\det(zI-M(\tau)),\qquad
\kappa=\det[1,\tau,\ldots,\tau^{17}].
\tag{5.3}
$$

Columns in the last determinant are coefficient vectors. An inverse is
defined exactly by $M(u)^{-1}\mathbf e_0$, or by its adjugate divided
by $\operatorname{Nm}(u)$. This specifies a rational polynomial circuit;
it does not select any ordering of roots. The nonvanishing facts needed
to define these functions over $k$ follow from Proposition 5.2.

**Proposition 5.2 (Finite coefficient and norm certificate).** At

Proof. All entries follow from the finite definitions (4.1)–(4.7) and

nonvanishing residues; it does not lift the modular zero identities

## 6. Reconstruction of all eighteen generic roots

**Theorem 6.1 (All-root original-source reconstruction).** Over an algebraic
closure of $k=\mathbb Q(p,v)$, $R$ has eighteen distinct roots.
At each root, (5.2) reconstructs a point of $X$ with exactly the
original acquired readings (1.3). Its gain is $g=H/U$, where $H$
is the entire polynomial (4.2), and its actual terminal $M$ response
is $\tau$. Every source over a suitable nonempty base open is
obtained exactly once in this way.

Consequently the acquired map from the original-source parent has
generic degree eighteen. This counts complete sources and their
uniquely determined entire gains and drifts; it does not count
square-slack signs and does not assert eighteen strict-real worlds.

Proof. Proposition 5.2 proves that $\rho$ and
$\operatorname{Disc}(\bar R)$ are nonzero in $k$. Thus the carrier
has degree eighteen and is reduced over an algebraic closure.
The resultants of $\bar R$ with $D,\alpha,a$ are nonzero, so
these three polynomials do not vanish at any generic carrier root.

Fix one such root $x$. The fixed resultant (4.7) is zero there.
Because $a\ne0$, the polynomial $f$ remains quadratic in $d$.
Writing its roots with multiplicity as $d_1,d_2$, its fixed
degree-$(2,4)$ resultant equals

$$
a^4K(d_1)K(d_2).
\tag{6.1}
$$

This formula remains valid if $K$ loses degree; relative to its
actual-degree resultant it merely includes the additional nonzero
power of $a$. Hence there is a finite common root of $f,K$.
Equation (4.6) forces every common root to equal
$\delta=-\beta/\alpha$. Thus

$$
f(x,\delta)=K(x,\delta)=0
\tag{6.2}
$$

at every generic root. In particular these equations hold in the
reduced algebra $B$. Their validity in characteristic zero follows
from the resultant and pseudo-remainder, independently of the modular
zero residues in (5.11).

All eleven norms in (5.7) are nonzero in $k$. Multiplication by
each element is invertible in $B$; after extending to the splitting
field, the element is therefore nonzero at every carrier root.
The definitions in (5.2) give $b,r,1-r,T,U,j,n\ne0$, and
$J=bj$, $N=bn$ will follow from the rank equation below.
They also give

$$
\delta U=-H_0,\qquad
jH_1=U(24-\delta),\qquad n=xU,\qquad r=n-j.
\tag{6.3}
$$

Put $B_0=294/5-2\delta-H_2$. The first equation of (6.2),
divided by the unit $5D^2$, says
$\delta H_2+H_0B_0=0$. Thus

$$
B_0U-H_2
=-\frac{\delta H_2+H_0B_0}{\delta}=0.
\tag{6.4}
$$

This proves clock two, since $T=(U+1)/2$ and
$B_0=2(147/5-\delta)-H_2$ imply
$\delta+TH_2/U=147/5$. The other first two readings are

$$
\delta+H_0/U=0,\qquad
\delta+jH_1/U=24.
\tag{6.5}
$$

For the source rank, put $Z=xH_1-(24-\delta)$.
The definitions imply $r=UZ/H_1$. A rational identity, valid
before imposing $f=K=0$, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
4\delta^2H_1^2\{r^2-(1+b)r+b(n-b)\}
={}&4H_0^2Z^2+2H_0(\delta-H_0)ZH_1\\
&-(H_0+\delta)\bigl(\delta+(1-2x)H_0\bigr)H_1^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.6}
$$

To obtain it, substitute $U=-H_0/\delta$, $b=(U-1)/2$,
$r=UZ/H_1$, and $n=xU$ and collect terms. Its right side
is $K/D^4$, hence zero by (6.2). Its multiplier is a unit.
It follows that

$$
b(n-r-b)=r(1-r),\qquad
j=n-r=b+r(1-r)/b.
\tag{6.7}
$$

Thus the reconstructed matrix is literally $P(r,b)$, not an
auxiliary rank-one parameter. In particular $J=bj$ and $N=bn$
are nonzero.

The interpolating values (4.2), divided by $U$, give the remaining
original readings:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta+ng(3)&=24,&\delta+Ug(4)&=18,\\
\delta+(U+n)g(5)&=p,&\delta+(2U+n)g(6)&=v.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.8}
$$

All equations use $g=H/U$, one entire polynomial of degree at
most three. Equations (6.5) exclude its identically zero value.
By the full source bridge, or directly by (2.3), these original
readings imply $\mathcal P=0$ with $e=\delta-24$. We have
therefore reconstructed a point of $X$ at every generic carrier root.
The trace (2.1) gives

$$
m=\delta+(3U+2n)g(7)=\delta+(3+2x)H_7=\tau.
\tag{6.9}
$$

All preceding identities are rational identities over the generic
base. There are only finitely many denominators and nonvanishing
norms, so they hold on a common nonempty Zariski-open subset of
$\mathbb A^2$. Shrink it by $S_F=0$, $p=24$, and $Z_E$.
This is still nonempty: (5.8) proves $S_F\not\equiv0$,
Proposition 3.2 makes $Z_E$ proper, and the base plane is
irreducible. Separate specializations certifying different nonzero
functions suffice; they need not be one common specialization.

Over this open, every original source is outside $E$ and so is an
$R$ root by Proposition 4.3. Equation (4.6) gives its unique drift
$\delta$. Its clock zero determines $U=-H_0/\delta$, clock one
determines $j$, and then $b,n,r,g$ are exactly (5.2).
Conversely each of the eighteen roots has just been lifted. Different
$x$ give different sources with complete worlds, because $x=n/U$
is a function of that world. Thus there are exactly eighteen points
in this generic source fibre.

Theorem 3.1 supplies an irreducible source surface and a dominant
map. In characteristic zero the degree of its function-field extension
equals the number of geometric points in a generic reduced finite
fibre. It is therefore eighteen. The three square roots of (2.9)
give a separate eightfold sign cover of each source; they are not
different complete worlds. No ordered real inequality was used in
the generic reconstruction. ∎

## 7. Generic injectivity of the original terminal response

**Theorem 7.1 (Generic injectivity to $(p,v,m)$).** There is a nonempty
Zariski-open subset $\Omega\subset\mathbb A^2_{\mathbb C}$ such
that every source of $X$ over $\Omega$ is one of the eighteen
sources of Theorem 6.1 and their eighteen actual terminal $M$ values
are pairwise distinct. In particular

$$
X\longrightarrow\mathbb A^3,\qquad W\longmapsto(p(W),v(W),m(W))
\tag{7.1}
$$

is generically one-to-one onto its image. The original terminal $M$
response is a primitive element of the degree-eighteen extension.

Proof. Over the splitting field let the eighteen simple roots of
$\bar R$ be $x_1,\ldots,x_{18}$. Evaluation identifies the carrier
algebra with the product of the eighteen root fields after scalar
extension. Consequently

$$
\chi(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{18}(z-\tau(x_i)).
\tag{7.2}
$$

Its discriminant is a nonzero rational function of $p,v$, by its
residue $30$ in (5.6). On a nonempty base open avoiding its
numerator and denominator the eighteen $\tau(x_i)$ are distinct.
Intersect this open with the one in Theorem 6.1 to obtain $\Omega$.

For the corresponding change of basis, multiply the coefficient
matrix of $1,\tau,\ldots,\tau^{17}$ by the Vandermonde matrix
of the $x_i$. The result is the Vandermonde matrix of the
$\tau(x_i)$. Squaring determinants gives the exact identity

$$
\operatorname{Disc}(\chi)
=\operatorname{Disc}(\bar R)\,\kappa^2.
\tag{7.3}
$$

The nonzero index $\kappa$ therefore says that these powers are a
basis of $B$. This finite-algebra separation argument is the one
already used in S, Theorem 4.3 and Lemma 9.9. Here Theorem 6.1
additionally identifies all eighteen algebraic points with original
sources, and Theorem 3.1 identifies the carrier's generic field
with the irreducible source function field. Hence this primitive
element is the actual response (6.9).

If two sources over $\Omega$ have equal $m$, (7.2) forces the
same $x$. The pseudo-remainder gives the same drift, and then
(5.2) gives the same $U,b,j,n,r$ and the same entire $g$.
They are the same complete world. This proves generic injectivity
and the primitive-element assertion on the source field. ∎

**Proposition 7.2 (A generic pair cannot satisfy the inverse guard).**
The guarded complex incidence $\mathcal Z_M$ has no point whose
base lies in $\Omega$.

Proof. Proposition 2.3 sends both labels of such a point to $X$.
Their common $p,v,m$ put them in the same fibre of (7.1).
Theorem 7.1 forces equal sources, gains and drifts. In particular
$t_1=t_2$ and $b_1=b_2$, so the first left side of (1.10)
equals $-1$, a contradiction. Equality of slack signs is unnecessary:
even distinct slack sign choices have zero source distance. ∎

## 8. The upper bound including saturation boundaries

**Theorem 8.1 (Proper complex projection and no dominating component).**
The entire corrected complex incidence satisfies

$$
\dim\mathcal B_M\le1.
\tag{8.1}
$$

The projected Zariski closure is unchanged if $\mathcal Z_M$ is
replaced by $V_{\mathbb C}(J_M)$. No irreducible component of that
saturated variety dominates the base plane.

Proof. By Proposition 7.2 the guarded projection is contained in
$\mathbb A^2\setminus\Omega$. This is a proper closed subset of
the plane, hence has dimension at most one. The same containment
holds for the projected closure.

To spell out the exceptional coverage, the complement includes the
image closures $Z_E$ of all four source strata (3.5), the divisor
$S_F=0$, and the zeros and poles of the finitely many rational
functions used in the carrier and reconstruction certificate. All
these sets are proper by Propositions 3.2 and 5.2. This finite union
is proper and closed. A point on any of them is retained in the
incidence; the argument only places its base in this proper union.
In particular an equal-$M$ pair with one exceptional label is
already accounted for by that label's image in $Z_E$.

For saturation, the standard localization identity over an algebraically
closed field is

$$
V_{\mathbb C}(I_M:Q_M^\infty)
=\overline{V_{\mathbb C}(I_M)\cap\{Q_M\ne0\}}^{\mathrm{Zar}}
=\overline{\mathcal Z_M}^{\mathrm{Zar}}.
\tag{8.2}
$$

It follows either from adjoining an inverse variable $h$ with
$hQ_M-1=0$ and eliminating it, or from localization and contraction
of ideals. See Cox–Little–O'Shea, *Ideals, Varieties, and Algorithms*,
the elimination theorem and the discussion of ideal quotients and
localization. For any continuous map $f$ and subset $S$,
$f(\overline S)\subseteq\overline{f(S)}$; this follows by
pulling back every closed set containing $f(S)$. The opposite
inclusion after taking closures follows from $S\subseteq\overline S$.
Applying this to the coordinate projection proves

$$
\overline{\operatorname{pr}_{p,v}V_{\mathbb C}(J_M)}^{\mathrm{Zar}}
=\mathcal B_M.
\tag{8.3}
$$

Thus $Q_M=0$ boundary points added by the saturation closure cannot
restore a two-dimensional projected image. A dominating irreducible
component would have full plane as its projected closure, contradicting
(8.1). ∎

**Proposition 8.2 (Scope of the exceptional bounds).** The bound (8.1)
includes singular $\Lambda=0$ sources, zero drift, $e=0$, zero
gain samples, repeated gain roots, drops of gain degree, vanishing
$n+U$ or $n+2U$, and all $Q_M=0$ points of the saturation
closure. No additional localization by any of these equations is
part of the corrected ideal.

Proof. The only global source localization in (1.11) is by $Q_M$,
in addition to the inverse guards already specified in $I_M$.
The source equations (2.5)–(2.7) contain no division by $e$ or
$\Lambda$, and construct all lower-degree gains. No gain-root
simplicity or global nonzero-sample hypothesis is imposed. The generic
reconstruction in Theorem 6.1 uses $H_0,H_1$ as justified units;
any failure of these unit conditions is retained in the proper base
complement of $\Omega$. Other permitted degeneracies over $\Omega$
are handled directly by that theorem. The sets
$d=0,e=0,n+U=0,n+2U=0$ were bounded by their images in
Proposition 3.2. Other failures of a generic denominator or certificate
lie on the proper base complement of $\Omega$; a source on such
a set was not asserted to have a defined pointwise generic inverse.
The identity (8.3) deals with the remaining closure boundary.
Thus the upper bound is a conclusion about the whole saturated
incidence, not an assertion that a nonzero generic chart is valid
at every point. ∎

Proof. For $x=(R,B)$ and $w(k)=(1,k)$ define the symmetric secant

$$

$$

\tag{21.6}
$$

To justify analyticity at the removed value, the fundamental theorem
of calculus gives

$$
\Phi(R,B,k,h)=\frac12\int_{-1}^{1}
 DF(x+thw(k))w(k)\,dt.
\tag{21.7}

$$

 +\frac{h^2}{6}D^3F(x)[w(k),w(k),w(k)]+O(h^4).
\tag{21.8}

change the entire-child independent-pair question (18.3).

Write $t=r/b$ and use the polynomial extension of the source matrix

$$
\widehat P(t,b)=
\begin{pmatrix}tb&b\\t(1-tb)&1-tb\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{24.1}
$$

When $t>0$, $b>0$ and $tb<1$, this is exactly the original strict
source $P(r,b)$ with $r=tb$. The extension to $b=0$ is used only for
limits, not to add worlds to the original domain.
For explicit polynomial inputs put

$$
\begin{gathered}
r=tb,\quad j=t+b(1-t^2),\quad n=j+tb,\quad T=1+b,\quad U=1+2b,\\
D=5\{(b^2+8b+4)jn-2TU(j+n)\},\quad S=(8b-1)(2+5b),\\
C_0=-24D+3Sjn,\quad C_1=3Sn,\\
C_2=3(49b+34)jn-54U(j+n),\quad C_3=3Sj,\\
B_5=-4C_0+15C_1-20C_2+10C_3,\\
B_6=-10C_0+36C_1-45C_2+20C_3,\\
B_7=-20C_0+70C_1-84C_2+35C_3,\\
N_p=-C_0+(U+n)B_5,\quad N_v=-C_0+(2U+n)B_6,\\
N_a=-C_0+(T+2j+4r)B_7,\quad N_d=-C_0,\\
N_m=-C_0+(3U+2n)B_7.
\end{gathered}
\tag{24.2}
$$

These are the supplied circuit (18.4)–(18.5), after the regular change
of coordinates. In particular $f=N_f/D$ for $f=p,v,a,d,m$.
No new gain or response convention is introduced.

For any polynomial $L(t,b)$ define polynomials $L_e,L_o$ by reduction
in the ring with $h^2=q$:

$$
L(X+Kh,Y+h)=L_e(X,Y,K,q)+hL_o(X,Y,K,q).
\tag{24.3}
$$

This definition requires no square root or division by $q$. Explicitly,
a monomial $c_{ij}t^ib^j$ contributes

$$
c_{ij}\binom{i}{a}\binom{j}{c}
X^{i-a}Y^{j-c}K^a
\begin{cases}
q^{(a+c)/2}&\text{to }L_e,\quad a+c\text{ even},\\
q^{(a+c-1)/2}&\text{to }L_o,\quad a+c\text{ odd},
\end{cases}
\tag{24.4}
$$

summed over $0\le a\le i$, $0\le c\le j$.
Apply this to $D,N_p,N_v,N_a,N_d,N_m$ and set

$$
\begin{gathered}
H_f=(N_f)_oD_e-(N_f)_eD_o,\\
H=\tfrac1{15}(H_p,H_v,H_a)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
\Delta=D_e^2-qD_o^2.
\end{gathered}
\tag{24.5}
$$

The three entries of $H$ have integer coefficients, total degree $22$,
and respectively $1144,1145,1145$ nonzero monomials in $(X,Y,K,q)$.
The numerical factor $15$ is constant and has no effect on their zeros.

For $q=h^2\ge0$ use the ordered endpoints

$$
t_\pm=X\pm Kh,\quad b_\pm=Y\pm h,\quad
r_\pm=t_\pm b_\pm,\qquad h=\sqrt q\ge0.
\tag{24.6}
$$

Whenever $D_\pm=D_e\pm hD_o\ne0$, direct multiplication gives

$$
\frac{N_f(t_+,b_+)}{D_+}-\frac{N_f(t_-,b_-)}{D_-}
=\frac{2hH_f}{\Delta}.
\tag{24.7}
$$

Consequently $H=0$ implies equal $p,v,a$. Their common values are

$$
\bar f(X,Y,K,q)=
\frac{(N_f)_eD_e-q(N_f)_oD_o}{\Delta},\qquad f=p,v,a.
\tag{24.8}
$$

Formula (24.8) is analytic also at $q=0$ when $\Delta\ne0$.
The divided drift and alternative-$M$ differences are $H_d/\Delta$
and $H_m/\Delta$, respectively. The equality equations impose no
common drift.

## 26. Analytic continuation, uniqueness joins and exact germ attachment

**Theorem 26.1 (The evaluated polynomial-secant continuation).**
There is a real analytic map

$$

$\widehat x_i(q)$ for $q\in I_i$. It is unique in each corresponding

analyticity on a neighborhood of each of its points. This is a
uniqueness assertion in the specified tubes, not an inventory of all
zeros of the polynomial equations.

The continuation is exactly the germ in Theorem 21.1 after changing
its parameter to the squared half-separation of the two $b$ coordinates.

*Proof.* For fixed $q\in I_i$ put $C(q)=J_i(\nu)^{-1}$.
The determinant bound in (25.4) makes this inverse legitimate.

whole ball the fundamental theorem of calculus, (25.5), (25.7) and

$$
\|I-C(q)H_x(x,q)\|_\infty\le\lambda_i<1.
\tag{26.2}
$$

The center displacement is at most $\eta_i$. By (25.9), $T_q$
maps the closed radius-$\rho$ ball strictly inside itself. The Banach
fixed-point theorem supplies a zero in its interior. Invertibility of
$C(q)$ makes fixed points exactly zeros of $H$.
The same derivative estimate implies uniqueness in the larger convex
radius-$2\rho$ ball. It also makes $C(q)H_x$ invertible by the Neumann
series, so the analytic implicit-function theorem applies at every zero.

Within a cell, continuity of the predictor and local uniqueness identify
these local analytic solutions. At a seam the predictor centers differ
by at most $2\cdot10^{-55}<\rho$. A root in one radius-$\rho$ ball
therefore lies in the other radius-$2\rho$ uniqueness ball. The two
roots agree; the same local implicit-function theorem identifies their
analytic extensions. This proves (26.1) on all twelve cells.
